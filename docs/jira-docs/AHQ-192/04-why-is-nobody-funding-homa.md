# Why Is Nobody Funding Homa?

> Research date: **2026-07-23**. Companion to [02-homa-detailed-research-document.md](02-homa-detailed-research-document.md) (see §14.4 for the economics that set up this question). Full sourced evidence: three research files in [supporting-docs/04-why-is-nobody-funding-homa-agent-research-docs/](supporting-docs/04-why-is-nobody-funding-homa-agent-research-docs/) — [Meta & hyperscalers](supporting-docs/04-why-is-nobody-funding-homa-agent-research-docs/research-meta-and-hyperscalers.md), [funding precedents](supporting-docs/04-why-is-nobody-funding-homa-agent-research-docs/research-funding-precedents.md), [tail economics](supporting-docs/04-why-is-nobody-funding-homa-agent-research-docs/research-tail-economics.md). Note: all three research runs were cut short by a spend limit; each file flags what was verified live vs not — treat marked items accordingly.

## The puzzle, stated precisely

If Homa could let a best-fit company (Meta being the canonical case) run latency-sensitive tiers at much higher utilisation — plausibly worth tens to low hundreds of $M/year — why is nobody paying even ~$1–2M to accelerate a patch series that has been stuck in review for 21 months with its author, working essentially alone, posting v19 into silence? Companies are not usually shy about spending $2M to get $100M.

## TL;DR — the verdict

**The puzzle mostly dissolves under evidence: it is not a market failure; it is the market pricing the opportunity roughly correctly.** Five findings do most of the work:

1. **Mainline was never the gate.** Hyperscalers run their own kernels (Google's carries ~9,000 out-of-tree patches). The Homa module has been runnable since 2020, with RHEL backports since March 2026. Anyone who wanted Homa's savings could have been collecting them for five years. **They aren't — and that is a demand signal, not a queue problem.** The definitive datapoint: a census of **all 638 Homa-related messages on netdev (2018–2026) found zero participation from any @meta.com/@fb.com address, ever** — including Meta's own employee who co-maintains the networking subsystem (his 11 messages in Homa threads are purely administrative).
2. **The headroom Homa would "free up" is insurance, not tail-fear** *(Steve's hypothesis — substantially confirmed)*. Facebook's own fabric measurements show ~1% *average* link utilisation with buffers two-thirds full at 10µs timescales: capacity headroom exists for microbursts, region-failure absorption, rollouts and growth lead-time — none of which a transport swap waives. If insurance independently caps utilisation, TCP's tail explosion above that level costs ~nothing, and the savings estimate evaporates. Add the incentive asymmetry: the engineer who proposes running the fleet hot owns 100% of the visible-outage career risk, while the capex saving (~0.15–0.3% of a hyperscaler's AI-era capex) accrues invisibly to finance. Nobody senior stakes a career on a rounding error.
3. **The cheap tail win was already harvested in software, a decade ago.** Hedged requests cut BigTable's P99.9 from 1,800ms to 74ms for +2% load ([The Tail at Scale, 2013](https://research.google/pubs/the-tail-at-scale/)) and are now commodity in gRPC/Envoy/Cassandra. Meta put memcached *gets* on UDP with sliding-window incast control in [2013](https://www.usenix.org/conference/nsdi13/technical-sessions/presentation/nishtala) — they dodged TCP on their hottest path thirteen years ago. And published tail-cause taxonomies blame the *host* (GC, daemons, C-states, scheduling), not the transport; no study was found attributing a dominant share of production end-to-end P99 to transport.
4. **Companies fund kernel transports they will use themselves, and overwhelmingly drop-in ones.** Every merged datacenter-transport success had a corporate sponsor who was its first production user: Microsoft→DCTCP, Google→BBR (merged in ~3 months; zero app changes), Intel→AF_XDP. The control case is MPTCP: IETF-standardised, shipped by Apple since 2013 — and it *still* took ~10 years plus dedicated Intel/Tessares engineers to reach mainline. Homa has no sponsor-user, and it isn't drop-in: applications must port to a message API (and the gRPC bridge is suspended).
5. **The µs-critical niches already bought point solutions, and the 2023–26 transport budget went to hardware.** ~70% of *all Azure traffic* runs on RDMA ([NSDI'23](https://www.usenix.org/conference/nsdi23/presentation/bai)); Google has Snap/Falcon, AWS has SRD. The industry's collective answer to "TCP is wrong for the datacenter" was the Ultra Ethernet Consortium — whose members include **Meta and Microsoft** — plus multi-billion-dollar DPU acquisitions. The ideas won; the money went to silicon. Ousterhout himself told LWN Homa's "real future may be inside the networking hardware."

## The seven hypotheses, ranked by how the evidence landed

| # | Hypothesis | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Headroom is insurance + risk asymmetry** (Steve's) | **Strongly supported** — utilisation data shows headroom is for bursts/failures/growth; transport can't waive it; incentive asymmetry compounds it |
| 2 | **Mainline isn't the gate; non-adoption is a demand signal** | **Strongly supported** — own-kernel fleets, 5 years of module availability, zero Meta engagement in 638 messages |
| 3 | **Tail win already harvested in software** (hedging, memcached-UDP) | **Strongly supported** — 2013-era mitigations, host-dominated tail taxonomies |
| 4 | **Drop-in-ness predicts funding; Homa isn't drop-in** | **Strongly supported** — DCTCP/BBR/AF_XDP vs the 10-year MPTCP control case; the adopter-side costs are itemised in [The adoption tax](#the-adoption-tax-what-using-homa-would-actually-cost-you) below |
| 5 | **µs niches already served; money went to AI/hardware transports** | **Strongly supported** — Azure RDMA at 70%, UEC membership, DPU spend |
| 6 | **Merged ≠ adopted; the kernel graveyard raises everyone's bar** | **Supported** — DCCP removed in 6.16 ("maintaining DCCP for a decade *without any real users* has become a burden… a playground for syzbot"); bcachefs merged 6.7 then *removed* 6.17 when the lone-maintainer model collapsed; RDS = Oracle-only + CVE stream. Maintainers rationally fear protocol #5 in that list; sponsors rationally discount "merged" as a milestone |
| 7 | **Public-goods dynamics** | **Supported by elimination** — the genuine beneficiaries of *mainline* Homa are many mid-size own-metal firms who don't participate in kernel development; no single one captures enough value to fund it. The only "funding" trace found is generic Stanford Platform Lab affiliate sponsorship (~$150–500K/yr lab membership — Google, Meta and Cisco among members — research money, not upstreaming muscle) |

## The adoption tax: what using Homa would actually cost you

A common assumption — worth killing explicitly — is that once Homa is in the kernel it becomes transparent, like a faster TCP you switch on. **It is nothing of the sort.** Even with a fully-performant mainline Homa, an adopter pays three layers of cost:

1. **Application code must change (the big one).** Homa is not a `SOCK_STREAM` you toggle; it's a different socket paradigm — message-oriented `sendmsg`/`recvmsg` with Homa-specific control structs, plus a receive path where the application pre-registers a buffer region and recycles 64KB bpages back to the kernel ([02 §6.2](02-homa-detailed-research-document.md#62-the-api-not-sockets-as-you-know-them)). Every service that touches sockets directly must be ported — against an API that is still churning under review ("the reviewing process is likely to result in API changes" — the module's own README). The designed escape hatch was hiding Homa inside RPC frameworks so applications change nothing: that path is **suspended** (grpc_homa, frozen since late 2023 at gRPC v1.57), and its measured payoff was only ~40% because gRPC's own overhead swamps the transport ([02 §14.2](02-homa-detailed-research-document.md#142-what-applications-must-change)).
2. **Infrastructure must be reconfigured.** Full benefit requires switch DSCP strict-priority queues, the homa_qdisc installed on every tx queue, jumbo frames end-to-end, NIC interrupt-coalescing tuning, and — on Intel NICs — the TCP-hijacking mode that some datacenter security middleware actively drops ([02 §9.4](02-homa-detailed-research-document.md#94-reproducing-what-a-future-task-would-need)).
3. **The ecosystem doesn't speak IP protocol 146.** Load balancers, firewalls, service meshes, eBPF tooling, packet-capture dashboards, observability stacks — all TCP-shaped. Every one of them is a gap the adopter must bridge or live without (the recurring Hacker News critique, and pure operational cost).

The contrast with the transports that *did* spread is stark and predictive. DCTCP and BBR are **one sysctl** — zero application changes, instant fleet rollout; BBR went from paper to mainline in about three months. MPTCP — which *does* change connection behaviour but still preserves the sockets API — took a decade even with Apple shipping it. The empirical law of kernel networking: **transparent wins spread; opt-in wins stall.** Homa is maximally opt-in.

Could Homa ever become transparent? The candidate on-ramps are: a revived, maintained RPC-framework integration (gRPC/Thrift — the realistic one, currently dead); service-mesh/sidecar adoption (pushes the port onto one vendor instead of every app team); or a stream-emulation shim over Homa (which would surrender part of the message-semantics benefit that justifies the switch). None exists today — so the adoption tax stands at full rate.

This section is the adopter-side face of hypothesis #4 above, and it completes the economics: the earlier sections showed the *benefit* is thinner than it looks (insurance headroom, already-harvested tails); this one shows the *cost* is a port-everything, reconfigure-the-fabric, retool-observability programme. Small numerator, large denominator — the non-adoption stops being mysterious from either side alone, and is doubly determined from both.

## The Meta case study (your Facebook framing, answered directly)

- **They have no private frontend transport to protect** — frontend is kernel TCP, AI backend is RoCEv2, edge is QUIC/mvfst. So "they secretly built their own Homa" is *not* the answer.
- **They demonstrably know about Homa** — the W1/W4 workload traces in Homa's own papers are Facebook's data. Yet in eight years, no Meta engineer has posted a single technical comment on it.
- **They could staff it trivially** — Meta employs a netdev co-maintainer (Jakub Kicinski) and the io_uring maintainer (Jens Axboe). Their kernel-networking investment went to io_uring zero-copy receive and hardware/standards bets (UEC founding member, in-house FBNIC) instead.
- **Why not spend $1–2M anyway?** Two reasons. First, money can't directly buy the scarce resource — netdev reviewer trust and bandwidth (the only sustained reviewer is Red Hat's Paolo Abeni; review is a gift economy, and a cheque doesn't queue-jump it). Meta could *staff* review — but that's a multi-year maintainership commitment to a protocol they'd then be expected to co-own (see: graveyard). Second, and decisively: per hypotheses 1–3, **the $100M/yr prize is mostly illusory for Meta specifically** — their headroom is insurance, their hot path left TCP in 2013, and their remaining tail problems live in the application layer. The rational value of Homa *to Meta* is close to zero. The market isn't failing to fund a $100M opportunity; the $100M was never really on the table for the companies capable of funding it.
- **And your "rent out a third of the servers" scenario**: unreachable via any transport swap. Networking is ~8–15% of datacenter TCO; servers are ~45–57%, provisioned on CPU/memory for application work. A transport that saved *fleet CPU* could touch the server line — but that's the offload/NIC-hardware story (Snap gets 3x Gbps/core; SRD/Falcon run in silicon), not kernel Homa, which spends *extra* cores on polling.

## What would falsify this verdict

Watch for: (a) any hyperscaler engineer doing sustained technical review of v20+; (b) a MAINTAINERS co-maintainer joining Ousterhout; (c) a cloud/hardware vendor productising Homa (MLE's FPGA work at SNIA SDC 2026 is the one live candidate); (d) grpc_homa revival or another zero-app-change on-ramp; (e) UEC/NIC vendors adopting Homa's receiver-driven scheduling into hardware specs — which would be the ideas winning *again* while the kernel protocol loses, and is frankly the most likely "success" ending on current evidence.

## The one-sentence answer

Nobody is funding Homa because the companies rich enough to fund it already solved their tail problems by other means (UDP, hedging, RDMA, custom silicon) and keep their headroom as insurance no transport can replace — while the many mid-size companies who *would* genuinely benefit from a free, in-kernel, commodity-Ethernet Homa are precisely the ones that never fund kernel plumbing; the result isn't a market failure so much as a public-goods gap that one retired professor is filling by hand.
