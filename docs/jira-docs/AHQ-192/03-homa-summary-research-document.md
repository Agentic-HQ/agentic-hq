## TL;DR — the main findings

1. **What it is.** Homa is a datacenter-only replacement for TCP: message-oriented (RPCs up to 1 MB, not byte streams), connectionless, **receiver-driven** (the receiver schedules incoming data via explicit grants), and SRPT-scheduled (shortest message first — enforced at the sender, at the receiver, and inside the switches via priority queues). Designed at Stanford by John Ousterhout's group (SIGCOMM 2018), it exists as a mature ~21K-line Linux kernel module. *(Details: [02 §1](02-homa-detailed-research-document.md#1-what-is-homa-in-one-page); full protocol mechanics: [02 §5](02-homa-detailed-research-document.md#5-the-homa-protocol-in-detail).)*

2. **The headline result is real and measured**: at high load with mixed message sizes, Homa's P99 small-message latency is **10–72x better than TCP's** (e.g. 58µs vs 4.2ms) — Homa's P99 is typically better than TCP's *mean*. The trade-offs: slightly lower peak bulk throughput, 2–3 extra cores if busy-polling is on, a new API (apps must port), and a max message size of 1 MB. *(Details + the measurement tables: [02 §8](02-homa-detailed-research-document.md#8-what-homa-makes-fast-what-it-makes-slower-and-what-limits-it).)*

3. **Status: NOT in the Linux kernel, and not close to a date.** After 21 months and **19 patch-series revisions** (v19, April 2026, "changes-requested"), nothing is merged and no maintainer has Acked it. The first upstream version is deliberately gutted — no grants, no priorities, no pacer, "about the same as TCP" by design — with all the performance machinery still to run the same review gauntlet afterwards. Working assumption: eventual merge plausible, timeline unknowable, realistically years to a *performant* mainline Homa. *(Details + full v1–v19 timeline: [02 §12](02-homa-detailed-research-document.md#12-upstream-status-as-of-2026-07-23).)*

4. **The people story is remarkable**: Ousterhout (Tcl/Tk, Raft, *A Philosophy of Software Design*), now retired from teaching, maintains the module essentially **single-handedly** — which is both why the code and its 6.5-year performance log are so good, and the project's biggest structural risk (and a real factor in kernel review hesitancy). In 2026 his patches are being reviewed partly by **netdev's AI review bot** — which found real bugs, including a kernel-stack info leak, and he publicly appreciated it. *(People and lineage: [02 §3](02-homa-detailed-research-document.md#3-history-and-people); the AI-review story: [02 §13.3](02-homa-detailed-research-document.md#133-the-ai-review-era-2026).)*

5. **Your LMAX instinct was right — and Homa is the perfect case study.** The same protocol runs at **P99 <15µs in user space** (DPDK, polling, LMAX-style run-to-completion) vs **~100µs as a kernel module**: a ~7x tail-latency price paid purely for the kernel's structure (packets scattered across cores by RSS, SoftIRQ handoffs, 2.5µs thread wakeups, 2–3x per-stage cache-coherency slowdowns). Ousterhout independently arrives at Martin Thompson's law — cache misses are the scaling limit. And the "buy one CPU fleet-wide" idea you floated is real, but happened one level down: hyperscalers put the transport in **one known NIC** deployed fleet-wide (AWS SRD in Nitro, Google Falcon in Intel IPUs). *(The kernel-vs-userspace experiment: [02 §11.3](02-homa-detailed-research-document.md#113-homa-as-the-natural-experiment-same-protocol-with-and-without-mechanical-sympathy); the whole LMAX section: [02 §11](02-homa-detailed-research-document.md#11-mechanical-sympathy-lmax-style-design-in-homa-and-its-alternatives).)*

6. **Best-in-class in 2026 is not TCP — but TCP is still what everyone deploys.** Hardware transports lead on raw numbers (RDMA-class NICs: ~2µs, hundreds of millions of msgs/sec; SRD/Falcon/Ultra Ethernet as the industry direction); eRPC holds the software record (~10M RPCs/sec/core, 2.3µs median); kernel TCP wins on zero-friction compatibility (~1M RPCs/sec/node, millisecond tails). Homa's niche: the only general-purpose, **in-kernel, plain-Ethernet** option with order-of-magnitude better tails than TCP — the attempt to give hyperscaler-grade tail latency to everyone who doesn't own a hyperscaler stack. *(Full comparison table: [02 §10](02-homa-detailed-research-document.md#10-alternatives-and-their-numbers); the best-in-class verdict: [02 §10.3](02-homa-detailed-research-document.md#103-so-whats-actually-best-in-class-in-2026).)*

7. **Success for this protocol is measured as *slowdown*, not messages/sec**: RTT ÷ ideal-RTT-for-that-size, at P50/P99, per message size, under ~80% sustained network load — deliberately punishing what operators care about (how bad small RPCs get when the network is busy). Raw msgs/sec-per-core records belong to the kernel-bypass world; kernel-resident transports are two to three orders of magnitude away from what the hardware can do, and that gap is the biggest number in the research. *(Benchmarks, workloads and proposed acceptance criteria: [02 §9](02-homa-detailed-research-document.md#9-benchmarks-how-success-is-measured).)*

8. **The money case: real but bounded — and strangely unfunded.** Homa's flat-tails-at-high-load property is a fleet-utilisation lever (fan-out math means one leg's P99 hits most user requests, so operators run tiers half-empty today). Best-placed beneficiaries: companies that own their DCs, run commodity Ethernet, live off small-RPC fan-out, and control one internal RPC framework — **Meta fits perfectly** (the benchmark traces are literally partly Facebook's). Plausible prize: tens to low hundreds of $M/year for the best-placed firms; billions only in aggregate. Which raises the puzzle — if that's true, why is nobody paying to accelerate a series stuck at v19? *(Economics: [02 §14.4](02-homa-detailed-research-document.md#144-the-economics-who-would-actually-save-money-and-how-much); the funding puzzle investigated: [04-why-is-nobody-funding-homa.md](04-why-is-nobody-funding-homa.md).)*

## Section summaries

### What Homa is ([02 §1](02-homa-detailed-research-document.md#1-what-is-homa-in-one-page))
Four decisions define it: messages not streams; receiver-driven congestion control (grants); SRPT everywhere including switch priority queues (DSCP); connectionless RPCs with at-most-once semantics. Payoff: tail latency. Costs: new API, ecosystem unfamiliarity, upstreaming grind.

### Network-stack refresher ([02 §2](02-homa-detailed-research-document.md#2-refresher-the-linux-network-stack-and-where-a-transport-lives))
The context needed for the rest: sk_buffs, TSO/GSO segmentation offload (built for TCP — which shaped Homa's wire format), the three-core receive path (NAPI/GRO → SoftIRQ → app, three uncoordinated schedulers), and the latency floors: ~ns in-memory, ~2µs kernel-bypass network, ~15–25µs kernel transport.

### History & people ([02 §3](02-homa-detailed-research-document.md#3-history-and-people))
Direct descendant of RAMCloud (5µs storage needed a 5µs transport; 2011 manifesto "It's Time for Low Latency"). SIGCOMM 2018 paper (Montazeri, Li, Alizadeh, Ousterhout); kernel module solo-built by Ousterhout since 2019. Trivia: nobody documents where the name "Homa" comes from (presumed: the Persian Huma bird).

### The case against TCP, and the pushback ([02 §4](02-homa-detailed-research-document.md#4-aims-the-case-against-tcp-in-the-datacenter--and-the-pushback))
Five indictments: streams, connections, fair-sharing, sender-driven buffer-filling congestion control, in-order delivery. Critics (Pepelnjak, APNIC, HN) attack the deployability and the rhetoric — ecosystem cost, prior art (RDMA/RoCE), rewrite cost — but notably not the measured tail-latency results. No formal academic rebuttal exists.

### The protocol ([02 §5](02-homa-detailed-research-document.md#5-the-homa-protocol-in-detail))
Unscheduled first-RTT bytes + granted everything-else; overcommitment (grant to ~8 messages at once — pure single-message granting wastes up to 40% of bandwidth); receiver-computed priority cutoffs pushed to senders; receiver-driven loss recovery that assumes timeouts mean overload, not loss; a pacer keeping NIC queues shallow so SRPT survives; a FIFO fraction preventing large-message starvation.

### Architecture & code ([02 §6](02-homa-detailed-research-document.md#6-architecture-and-code-deep-dive-homamodule))
The section to read before touching the code — module map, two mermaid diagrams (component + RPC sequence). Highlights: the receive path lands data in *application-registered* memory (64KB bpages the app recycles); locking is per-RPC, not per-socket, with a deliberately inverted lock order; **the wire header is a byte-for-byte TCP costume** so NICs will hardware-segment it, escalating to full "TCP hijacking" (send as real TCP, steal packets back before TCP sees them); Homa schedules *cores*, not just packets (custom GRO→SoftIRQ steering, Gen2/Gen3); the June 2024 skb-frag refactor reclaimed ~3 cores; the Jan 2026 homa_qdisc makes TCP coexistence a solved, measured problem.

### Six years of performance engineering ([02 §7](02-homa-detailed-research-document.md#7-six-years-of-performance-engineering-what-actually-made-it-fast))
perf.txt = 71 dated investigations, one person's lab notebook. Recurring lessons: the platform, not the protocol, is the bottleneck; measure-first (several "obviously good" ideas measured and removed); the 100 Gbps wall is NIC queues and memory bandwidth (where switch priorities stop mattering); hardware/kernel versions move numbers as much as design (SMIs freezing all cores 200–300µs; softirqd priority inversion causing 5–7ms P999; kernel 4.15→5.17 regressing RTT ~25%).

### What's fast, what isn't ([02 §8](02-homa-detailed-research-document.md#8-what-homa-makes-fast-what-it-makes-slower-and-what-limits-it))
Dramatically better: small-message P99 under load (the headline tables live here). Similar/worse: peak bulk throughput (72–75 vs TCP's 78–79 Gbps at 100G), CPU with polling on (+2–3 cores; polling off = slightly cheaper than TCP), best-case unloaded latency (only ~1.5x better), large messages under overload (SRPT's dark side, FIFO-bounded).

### Benchmarks & success metrics ([02 §9](02-homa-detailed-research-document.md#9-benchmarks-how-success-is-measured))
Slowdown vs message size at P50/P99, workloads W1–W5 from real datacenter traces (Facebook memcached, Google search, Google aggregate, Facebook Hadoop, DCTCP websearch), 80% load, tools cp_node/cp_vs_tcp/cp_both + /proc/net/homa_metrics + kernel timetraces. §9.5 proposes concrete acceptance criteria for "Homa succeeded". Reproduction needs a multi-node Linux cluster — single-machine runs are meaningless.

### Alternatives ([02 §10](02-homa-detailed-research-document.md#10-alternatives-and-their-numbers))
Full comparison table with sourced numbers: kernel TCP, DCTCP/BBR, RDMA/RoCEv2 (and its PFC horror stories), AWS SRD, eRPC, Demikernel, Seastar, Google Snap/Swift/Falcon, QUIC (wrong tool, measured), and the research family (pHost, NDP, Aeolus, dcPIM, SIRD). Bottom line in §10.3: hardware transports for numbers, eRPC-style bypass for software records, TCP for deployability — Homa bets on the empty quadrant: in-kernel + commodity Ethernet + good tails.

### Mechanical sympathy — the LMAX section ([02 §11](02-homa-detailed-research-document.md#11-mechanical-sympathy-lmax-style-design-in-homa-and-its-alternatives))
LMAX Disruptor claims verified against the primary sources (26M ops/sec inter-thread; 52ns mean handoff; 6M TPS single-threaded business logic; "mechanical sympathy" = Thompson via Jackie Stewart). Why nanoseconds can't cross a network but the cost model does; Homa as the natural experiment (same protocol: 15µs bypass vs 100µs kernel P99); the run-to-completion school (eRPC, Seastar, DPDK, Snap, Shenango/Caladan, io_uring-as-a-Disruptor); "one CPU fleet-wide" corrected to "one NIC fleet-wide" (SRD, Falcon, BlueField); and cache-line-level evidence from Homa's own structs.

### Upstream status ([02 §12](02-homa-detailed-research-document.md#12-upstream-status-as-of-2026-07-23)) and the sociology ([02 §13](02-homa-detailed-research-document.md#13-the-sociology-of-upstreaming-what-the-kernel-process-does-to-a-research-protocol))
Nothing merged (verified against mainline/net-next/linux-next); v19 at "changes-requested"; v20 visibly in prep. The review history is a case study: architectural objections (locking/RCU, "quite far from mergeable") → infrastructure objections (pacer deleted, get_cycles banned — a 6ns-per-call argument worth 0.2 cores at Homa's 21M clock reads/sec) → mechanical cleanups → AI-bot reviews finding real bugs. The deeper blockers: reviewer bandwidth, no visible corporate user, single-academic-maintainer risk.

### Use cases & ecosystem ([02 §14](02-homa-detailed-research-document.md#14-use-cases-and-ecosystem))
RPC-heavy microservices and storage are the target; apps must port (or hide Homa in frameworks — grpc_homa showed ~40% gains but is suspended; gRPC's own 60µs of overhead swamps the transport). No production deployment anywhere yet; hyperscalers built their own Homa-shaped transports instead — validating the ideas while competing with the protocol. **§14.4** adds the economics: how flat tails convert to fleet utilisation and money, the five-trait ideal-beneficiary profile (Meta is the canonical fit), and the millions-vs-billions verdict — with the "why is nobody funding it, then?" question handed off to the [04 document](04-why-is-nobody-funding-homa.md).
