## 1. LMAX Disruptor primer (verified facts)

### 1.1 What LMAX built, and who

- LMAX (London Multi-Asset Exchange) built "a new retail financial trading platform" on the JVM; its architecture is described in Martin Fowler's July 2011 article [The LMAX Architecture](https://martinfowler.com/articles/lmax.html) (publication date "12 July 2011" is on the page).
- Fowler names the Disruptor's creators: "Martin Thompson, Mike Barker, and Dave Farley" designed the Disruptor component ([Fowler, The LMAX Architecture](https://martinfowler.com/articles/lmax.html)).
- The Disruptor technical paper lists five authors: **Martin Thompson, Dave Farley, Michael Barker, Patricia Gee, and Andrew Stewart** (May 2011) ([LMAX Disruptor technical paper](https://lmax-exchange.github.io/disruptor/disruptor.html)).
- The GitHub repo describes it as "A High Performance Inter-Thread Messaging Library", Apache-2.0 licensed ([LMAX-Exchange/disruptor](https://github.com/LMAX-Exchange/disruptor)). The top contributor by a wide margin is `mikeb01` (423 commits vs 347 for `mjpt777`, per the [GitHub contributors API](https://api.github.com/repos/LMAX-Exchange/disruptor/contributors?per_page=5)) — `mikeb01` is Mike Barker and `mjpt777` is Martin Thompson (see [Mike Barker on the LMAX technology blog](https://technology.lmax.com/authors/mike-barker/) and [SE Radio episode 254 with Mike Barker on the LMAX architecture](https://se-radio.net/2016/04/se-radio-episode-254-mike-barker-on-the-lmax-architecture/)).
- The Disruptor was open-sourced in June 2011: ["Disruptor, Now Open Source!" (LMAX blog, 22 June 2011)](https://technology.lmax.com/posts/disruptor-now-open-source/).
- Before that, Thompson and Barker presented ["LMAX — How to Do 100K TPS at Less than 1ms Latency"](https://www.infoq.com/presentations/LMAX-Disruptor-100K-TPS-at-Less-than-1ms-Latency) at QCon San Francisco (Dec 2010) — i.e., the *production system* claim was 100K transactions/sec at <1 ms latency; the 6M/25M figures below are benchmark numbers for specific components.

### 1.2 "Mechanical sympathy" — the term

- Martin Thompson coined the software use of the term on his blog ["Why Mechanical Sympathy" (15 July 2011)](https://mechanical-sympathy.blogspot.com/2011/07/why-mechanical-sympathy.html). The phrase is borrowed from three-time F1 world champion **Jackie Stewart**, who held that the best drivers understand their machines well enough to work *with* them. Thompson's software meaning: understand how the hardware actually works (caches, memory, pipelines) and design software in harmony with it.

### 1.3 The verified performance claims

**The "25 million messages/sec and <50 ns" claim** — the exact sentence in the [Disruptor technical paper](https://lmax-exchange.github.io/disruptor/disruptor.html) is:

> "On processors at moderate clock rates we have seen over 25 million messages per second and latencies lower than 50 nanoseconds."

What was actually measured (context matters a lot):

- This is **inter-thread messaging on one machine** — cost of handing a reference between threads through the ring buffer — described in the paper as "very close to the theoretical limit of a modern processor to exchange data between cores". It is *not* an end-to-end system or network number.
- The 25M msgs/sec figure corresponds to the paper's Table 2 unicast (1 producer → 1 consumer) benchmark: **25,998,336 ops/sec on a 2.8 GHz Nehalem (Windows 7)** and **22,381,378 ops/sec on a 2.2 GHz Sandy Bridge (Linux)**, vs ~5.3M and ~4.1M ops/sec respectively for Java's `ArrayBlockingQueue` — roughly the "8 times more throughput" the paper claims ([Disruptor technical paper, Table 2](https://lmax-exchange.github.io/disruptor/disruptor.html)).
- The <50 ns latency figure corresponds to Table 4 (three-stage pipeline, 2.2 GHz Core i7, Ubuntu): **Disruptor mean latency 52 ns vs 32,757 ns for `ArrayBlockingQueue`** — the "3 orders of magnitude lower" mean-latency claim. Tail: Disruptor 99th percentile 128 ns, 99.99th percentile 8,192 ns, max 175,567 ns; ABQ 99th percentile 2,097,152 ns, max 5,069,086 ns ([Disruptor technical paper, Table 4](https://lmax-exchange.github.io/disruptor/disruptor.html)). Note the headline says "lower than 50 ns" while the published table mean is 52 ns — same order, slightly different runs; treat the headline as ≈50 ns.

**The "6 million TPS on one thread" claim** — exact wording in [Fowler's LMAX article](https://martinfowler.com/articles/lmax.html): the Business Logic Processor is "a thread that will process 6 million orders per second using commodity hardware", and "The 6 million TPS benchmark was measured on a 3Ghz dual-socket quad-core Nehalem based Dell server with 32GB RAM." This is the single-threaded, in-memory matching engine benchmark — all business logic on **one** thread, with I/O concerns (unmarshalling, journalling, replication) handled by other threads via Disruptors.

### 1.4 Core design elements (from the [technical paper](https://lmax-exchange.github.io/disruptor/disruptor.html) unless noted)

- **Origin problem**: LMAX found "the latency costs, when using queues … were in the same order of magnitude as the cost of IO operations to disk" — the queue itself was the bottleneck.
- **Pre-allocated ring buffer**: "All memory for the ring buffer is pre-allocated on start up" — no allocation/GC on the hot path, contiguous layout for cache friendliness; ring size a power of 2 so "a bit mask of size minus one" replaces the modulo.
- **Single-writer principle**: "The ideal algorithm would be one with only a single thread owning all writes to a single resource with other threads reading the results." Thompson's standalone post [The Single Writer Principle (22 Sept 2011)](https://mechanical-sympathy.blogspot.com/2011/09/single-writer-principle.html) quantifies why: incrementing a counter 500M times on a 2.4 GHz Westmere takes **300 ms on one thread**, **~18,000 ms with two threads using CAS**, **~118,000 ms with two threads using a lock**; and "Managing cache misses is the single largest limitation to scaling the performance of our current generation of CPUs."
- **No locks; minimal CAS**: consumers follow a sequence counter; with a single producer there is no contended write at all — "memory barrier to read the cursor" suffices, no CAS needed on the fast path.
- **Cache-line padding / false sharing**: "Caches are organised into cache-lines that are typically 32–256 bytes in size, the most common being 64 bytes"; if two independent variables share a line, "they present the same problems of write contention as if they were a single variable … known as 'false sharing'" — hence padded sequence counters.
- **Memory barriers**: in Java, "read and write of a volatile field implements the read and write barriers respectively"; the design relies on ordered publication of the sequence cursor rather than mutual exclusion.
- **Wait strategies incl. busy-spin**: consumers can "wait on a condition variable … or loop checking the cursor … with or without a thread yield by trading CPU resource against latency" — i.e., busy-spin polling is an explicit latency-optimised option.
- **Batching effect**: a lagging consumer "can process up to that sequence without getting involved in the concurrency mechanisms", so latency "remains almost flat until saturation occurs of the memory sub-system" — versus queues' exponential latency growth near saturation.
- **Separation of concerns**: the paper argues queues conflate storage, producer coordination, and consumer notification; the Disruptor separates them around one ring.

**One-paragraph takeaway**: the Disruptor school = single writer per datum, pre-allocated fixed rings, no locks, padded/aligned state, explicit memory ordering, polling wait strategies, one big single-threaded state machine for the actual business logic, and everything sized so the working set stays in cache.

## 2. Physics calibration: why the Disruptor's numbers cannot transfer across machines

The Disruptor's 50 ns / 25M msg/s numbers are **core-to-core cache-coherency numbers inside one coherence domain**. Between machines you pay, at minimum: NIC processing + PCIe transaction + serialisation onto the wire + propagation + switch hops — a floor that is three to four orders of magnitude above 50 ns.

Sourced calibration points:

- **RDMA / kernel-bypass floor (~1–2 µs)**: "A very basic remote direct memory access (RDMA) operation in a fast data-center network takes approximately 2 µs" — Barroso, Marty, Patterson, Ranganathan, ["Attack of the Killer Microseconds", CACM 2017](https://cacm.acm.org/research/attack-of-the-killer-microseconds/) (PDF, extracted text). The same article shows how "a 2 µs fabric can, through a cumulative set of software overheads, turn into a nearly 100 µs datacenter fabric", and that "using a full-fledged TCP/IP stack rather than the RDMA-based transport adds to the final overhead that exceeds 75 µs."
- **Measured kernel-bypass RPC RTTs**: eRPC's "median latency on CX5 is only 2.3 µs" (small RPCs, dual-port 40 GbE ConnectX-5, lossy Ethernet); 2.1 µs on ConnectX-3 InfiniBand; 3.7 µs on 25 GbE ConnectX-4 Lx — [eRPC paper, NSDI'19 (arXiv HTML)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1806.00680).
- **Measured kernel-bypass Homa RTT**: "a 100-byte echo RPC takes 4.7 µs in an unloaded network; at 80% network load, the 99th-percentile latency was about 14 µs" (RAMCloud + DPDK, 10 Gbps network) — [Homa SIGCOMM'18 paper (arXiv HTML)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1803.09615).
- **Kernel TCP floor (~15–30 µs RTT)**: on CloudLab xl170 (Xeon E5-2640 v4 class, 25 Gbps ConnectX-4), best-case small-message round-trips were **15.1 µs for Homa/Linux vs 23.4/24.1 µs for TCP/DCTCP** — [Homa/Linux ATC'21 paper (USENIX PDF)](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/atc21-ousterhout.pdf) (PDF, extracted text; corroborated by [HomaModule perf.txt](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/PlatformLab/HomaModule/master/perf.txt): "Best-case median RTT for short messages approximately 12 µs" on xl170, July 2020).
- Ousterhout at netdev 0x16: hardware RTTs are "five or ten microseconds", TCP short-message latencies under load reach "the millisecond range", so "we're one to two orders of magnitude off — or more" ([LWN coverage part 1](https://lwn.net/Articles/913260/)); and gRPC-over-TCP round trip measured at **90 µs**, "two-thirds of that time spent in the gRPC layers on the client and server (30 µs each)" ([LWN coverage part 2](https://lwn.net/Articles/914030/)).
- **The comparison that matters**: the same protocol (Homa), same author's group: **P99 ~14–15 µs in user space with kernel bypass vs ~100 µs in the Linux kernel** under the same W4-style workloads ("In the RAMCloud storage project, Stanford researchers implemented Homa in user space and were able to achieve 99th percentile round-trip latencies of 14 µs, while the tail latency for Homa on Linux is 100 µs" — [LWN part 2](https://lwn.net/Articles/914030/)).

So: LMAX's *numbers* don't transfer (ns → µs, three orders of magnitude), but the *failure mode LMAX identified* — contention, cache misses, queuing, context switches costing more than the "real work" — transfers exactly. In the microsecond regime, "overhead components often equal or exceed the latency of the I/O device itself" ([Killer Microseconds](https://cacm.acm.org/research/attack-of-the-killer-microseconds/), PDF extracted text).

## 3. Where LMAX-style thinking DID transfer: the run-to-completion school

Each of these systems independently rediscovered or consciously applied Disruptor-style rules: one thread per core, single-writer/per-core state, no fast-path locks, pre-allocated buffers, busy-polling, batching, cache-conscious layout.

### 3.1 eRPC (NSDI'19)

- Paper: [Datacenter RPCs can be General and Fast](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.00680), Anuj Kalia, Michael Kaminsky, David G. Andersen; NSDI'19 ([USENIX page](https://www.usenix.org/conference/nsdi19/presentation/kalia)). Won best paper.
- Headline numbers (abstract, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.00680)): "one CPU core can handle up to 10 million small RPCs per second, or send large messages at 75 Gbps", with "5.5 microseconds of replication latency on lossy Ethernet" for 3-way state-machine replication.
- Latency: "eRPC's median latency on CX5 is only 2.3 µs" for small RPCs ([arXiv HTML](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1806.00680)); clusters used: 56 Gbps ConnectX-3/InfiniBand (Xeon E5-2650), 25 GbE ConnectX-4 Lx on a 100-node lossy-Ethernet cluster (Xeon E5-2640 v4), 40 GbE ConnectX-5 (Xeon E5-2697/2683). The 10M RPCs/s/core context: on the CX4 cluster "each thread issues 5 million requests per second" bidirectionally ([arXiv HTML](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1806.00680)).
- LMAX-flavoured design ([arXiv HTML](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1806.00680)):
  - "Each user thread that sends or receives RPCs creates an exclusive Rpc endpoint" and "a session is a one-to-one connection between two Rpc endpoints, i.e., two user threads" — per-thread ownership, no shared session state (single-writer).
  - Dispatch threads "must periodically run their Rpc endpoint's event loop" — polling event loop; "we use userspace networking with polling" (no interrupts); request handlers can run to completion in the dispatch thread.
  - "A recurring theme in eRPC's design is that we optimize for the common case" — small RPCs, no congestion, handlers in the dispatch thread.
  - Cache-consciousness at the NIC interface: "we use features of current NICs (e.g., multi-packet RQ descriptors …) in novel ways to guarantee a constant NIC memory footprint per CPU core" — bounding per-core NIC state so it stays cache-resident.

### 3.2 Seastar / ScyllaDB (shared-nothing shards)

- From the official [Seastar tutorial](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/scylladb/seastar/master/doc/tutorial.md): "Since modern multi-core and multi-socket machines have steep penalties for sharing data between cores (atomic instructions, cache line bouncing and memory fences), Seastar programs use the share-nothing programming model, i.e., the available memory is divided between the cores, each core works on data in its own part of memory, and communication between cores happens via explicit message passing." Also: "Each thread is preallocated with a large piece of memory (on the same NUMA node it is running on), and uses only that memory for its allocations."
- This is the Disruptor position taken to its logical conclusion: not just single-writer per variable, but single-owner core per data shard, with cross-core traffic made explicit (message queues) instead of accidental (coherence misses).
- ScyllaDB ships this as "shard-per-core": each shard gets its own CPU, memory, network and storage allocation, "it never context switches, it never waits" ([ScyllaDB shard-per-core architecture page](https://www.scylladb.com/product/technology/shard-per-core-architecture/), [Why ScyllaDB's Shard Per Core Architecture Matters](https://www.scylladb.com/2024/10/21/why-scylladbs-shard-per-core-architecture-matters/)).

### 3.3 DPDK poll-mode drivers

- From the [DPDK Poll Mode Driver programmer's guide](https://doc.dpdk.org/guides-20.11/prog_guide/poll_mode_drv.html):
  - No interrupts: "A PMD accesses the RX and TX descriptors directly without any interrupts … to quickly receive, process and deliver packets in the user's application."
  - Run-to-completion as a named model: "In the run-to-completion model, a specific port's RX descriptor ring is polled for packets through an API. Packets are then processed on the same core and placed on a port's TX descriptor ring."
  - Per-core, lock-free: "PMDs are designed to work with per-core private resources as much as possible. For example, a PMD maintains a separate transmit queue per-core, per-port"; "Every receive queue of a port is assigned to and polled by a single logical core (lcore)"; "Avoiding lock contention is a key issue in a multi-core environment."
  - Batching to amortise: burst APIs "share among multiple packets the un-amortized cost" of a per-packet call and exploit "burst-oriented hardware features (prefetch data in cache, use of NIC head/tail registers)."
- Hugepages requirement — the TLB is a cache too: "By using hugepage allocations, performance is increased since fewer pages are needed, and therefore less Translation Lookaside Buffers … which reduce the time it takes to translate a virtual page address to a physical page address" ([DPDK Linux getting-started guide, system requirements](https://doc.dpdk.org/guides/linux_gsg/sys_reqs.html)).
- NIC descriptor rings themselves are single-producer/single-consumer circular buffers with head/tail indices — the same shape as a Disruptor ring, in silicon-adjacent form.

### 3.4 Google Snap / Pony Express (SOSP'19)

- Paper: [Snap: a Microkernel Approach to Host Networking](https://research.google/pubs/snap-a-microkernel-approach-to-host-networking/), Marty, de Kruijf, Adriaens, … Vahdat (Google), SOSP'19 ([PDF](https://courses.grainger.illinois.edu/cs598hpn/fa2020/papers/snap.pdf)).
- Engines = single-threaded state machines (PDF, extracted text from the [paper](https://courses.grainger.illinois.edu/cs598hpn/fa2020/papers/snap.pdf)): "Engines are stateful, single-threaded tasks that are scheduled and run by a Snap engine scheduling runtime"; "lock-free communication occurs over memory-mapped regions shared with the input or output"; "The Pony Express engine always spins."
- Scheduling modes ([the morning paper summary](https://blog.acolyer.org/2019/11/11/snap-networking/)): dedicated cores ("an engine is pinned to a dedicated hyperthread on which no other work can run"), spreading engines (interrupt-driven when idle), and compacting engines ("collapses work onto as few cores as possible").
- **MicroQuanta**: a custom kernel scheduling class — "A MicroQuanta thread runs for a configurable runtime out of every period time units" and "uses only per-CPU high-resolution timers. This allows scalable time slicing at microsecond granularity" ([Snap PDF](https://courses.grainger.illinois.edu/cs598hpn/fa2020/papers/snap.pdf), extracted text).
- Numbers: "At an offered load of 80 Gbps, Snap is over 3x more efficient than TCP" (Gbps/core); "up to 5 million remote memory accesses per second served with a single Snap/Pony dedicated core"; "configuring the application to spin-poll on the Pony Express completion queue reduces latency to less than 10 µsecs" ([Snap PDF](https://courses.grainger.illinois.edu/cs598hpn/fa2020/papers/snap.pdf), extracted text; the 3x Gbps/core and 5M ops/s figures corroborated by [acolyer's summary](https://blog.acolyer.org/2019/11/11/snap-networking/): Snap/Pony 38 Gbps on 1.05 cores vs baseline kernel TCP 22 Gbps on 1.2 cores).

### 3.5 Shenango / Caladan (microsecond core reallocation)

- [Shenango (NSDI'19, Amy Ousterhout et al.)](https://www.usenix.org/conference/nsdi19/presentation/ousterhout) reallocates cores across applications "at very fine granularity — every 5 µs" so latency-sensitive apps can busy-poll without wasting whole cores ([paper PDF](http://www.abelay.me/data/shenango_nsdi19.pdf)).
- [Caladan (OSDI'20)](https://amyousterhout.com/papers/caladan_osdi20.pdf) extends this to interference control: "Caladan is a system that enables servers in datacenters to simultaneously provide low tail latency and high CPU efficiency, by rapidly reallocating cores across applications" ([caladan GitHub README](https://github.com/shenango/caladan)), with a dedicated scheduler core (IOKernel) and kernel-bypass networking; runtime cores can use "directpath" to talk to the NIC directly ([README](https://github.com/shenango/caladan)).
- Relevance to the school: these systems accept the busy-polling, core-dedication model as *the* way to get microsecond latency, then attack its main cost (wasted cycles) by making core allocation itself microsecond-fast.

### 3.6 Demikernel (SOSP'21)

- [The Demikernel Datapath OS Architecture for Microsecond-scale Datacenter Systems](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/the-demikernel-datapath-os-architecture-for-microsecond-scale-datacenter-systems/) (Irene Zhang et al., SOSP'21; [ACM DOI](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3477132.3483569)): "Datacenter systems and I/O devices now run at single-digit microsecond latencies, requiring ns-scale operating systems"; Demikernel is "a flexible datapath OS and architecture designed for heterogenous kernel-bypass devices and µs-scale datacenter systems", letting ported applications run "across heterogenous kernel-bypass devices [RDMA, DPDK] with ns-scale overheads."
- Relevance: makes explicit the arithmetic that when the wire is ~2 µs, the OS is only allowed nanoseconds — i.e., the OS datapath itself must be built with Disruptor-grade cost discipline.

### 3.7 io_uring — a Disruptor between user space and the kernel

- [LWN, "Ringing in a new asynchronous I/O API" (Jonathan Corbet, 15 Jan 2019)](https://lwn.net/Articles/776703/) describes io_uring's two rings, mmap'd into shared memory ("the return value from io_uring_setup() is a file descriptor that can then be passed to mmap()"), with strict single-producer/single-consumer ownership: on the submission queue "user-space code adds an entry by putting its index into array[r.tail] and incrementing the tail pointer; only the kernel side should change r.head", and on the completion queue "user space should only change r.head."
- With `IORING_SETUP_SQPOLL`, "a call to io_uring_enter() will kick off a kernel thread that will occasionally poll the submission queue and automatically submit any requests found there" — I/O "with no further system calls" ([LWN](https://lwn.net/Articles/776703/)).
- Structural comparison to the Disruptor: pre-allocated fixed-size ring, exactly one writer per index (single-writer principle), progress communicated by publishing head/tail counters with memory barriers rather than locks, optional busy-poll consumer. Jens Axboe's design document ["Efficient IO with io_uring" (kernel.dk/io_uring.pdf)](https://kernel.dk/io_uring.pdf) covers the required read/write barriers when updating head/tail (see gaps — this PDF could not be fetched here, but the LWN article documents the same ownership rules).

## 4. CPU-model-specific vs microarchitecture-aware

### 4.1 Does anyone ship transports compiled for ONE specific CPU model? Essentially no.

- **DPDK** exposes the choice but defaults distribution builds to portability: "`-Dplatform=native` will tailor the configuration to the build machine" (sets `-march=native`), while "`-Dplatform=generic` will use configuration that works on all machines of the same architecture" using "a common minimal baseline" ([DPDK build guide](https://doc.dpdk.org/guides/linux_gsg/build_dpdk.html)). So operators *can* build for their exact CPU, but shipped/distro binaries are generic — nobody publishes "this transport only runs on Xeon E5-2697 v4".
- **ScyllaDB** ships generic binaries and instead does *runtime* machine-specific tuning: `perftune.py` "handles network and disk tuning, reassigning IRQs to specific CPU cores and freeing the other cores for ScyllaDB's exclusive use", with NUMA-aware shard memory ([ScyllaDB perftune docs](https://docs.scylladb.com/manual/stable/operating-scylla/admin-tools/perftune.html), [perftune.py source](https://github.com/scylladb/seastar/blob/master/scripts/perftune.py)).
- What actually gets shipped is **microarchitecture-awareness**, not model-specificity:
  - **Cache-line padding / 64-byte alignment** (Disruptor: false-sharing avoidance, [technical paper](https://lmax-exchange.github.io/disruptor/disruptor.html); DPDK: cache-aligned per-core rings and burst prefetching, [PMD guide](https://doc.dpdk.org/guides-20.11/prog_guide/poll_mode_drv.html)).
  - **NUMA pinning** (Seastar preallocates each shard's memory "on the same NUMA node it is running on", [tutorial](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/scylladb/seastar/master/doc/tutorial.md)).
  - **Intel DDIO awareness**: DDIO makes "I/O devices perform DMA directly to/from Last Level Cache (LLC) rather than system memory", but "DDIO only uses a subset of LLC ways, by default two ways" (2/11 ≈ 18% of LLC on Skylake), and "going beyond ~75 Gbps can cause DDIO to become a bottleneck" — [Farshin et al., "Reexamining Direct Cache Access…", ATC'20](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/atc20-farshin.pdf) (PDF, extracted text; see also [Intel's DDIO performance-monitoring article](https://www.intel.com/content/www/us/en/developer/articles/technical/ddio-analysis-performance-monitoring.html)). High-end stacks size RX descriptor rings and touch-order so packets are still LLC-resident when the consuming core reads them.
  - **aRFS steering**: "Accelerated RFS is … a hardware-accelerated load balancing mechanism that uses soft state to steer flows based on where the application thread consuming the packets of each flow is running", which "should perform better than RFS since packets are sent directly to a CPU local to the thread consuming the data" ([Linux kernel scaling.txt](https://www.kernel.org/doc/Documentation/networking/scaling.txt)) — i.e., steer the packet to the cache that already holds the flow state (data-locality, the network analogue of the Disruptor keeping consumer state hot).

### 4.2 The real "homogeneous special hardware" strategy: it happens at the NIC, not the CPU

The hyperscalers *did* pursue the "we control all the hardware, so specialise for it" idea — but they specialised the **NIC/transport silicon**, not the CPU binary:

- **AWS Nitro / SRD**: SRD (Scalable Reliable Datagram) is implemented in the Nitro card. Design rationale: "Our goal was to have SRD as close as possible to the physical network layer and to avoid performance noise injected by the host OS and hypervisor" — [Shalev, Ayoub, Bshara, Sabbag (Annapurna Labs/AWS), "A Cloud-Optimized Transport Protocol for Elastic and Scalable HPC", IEEE Micro 2020](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/9167399/) ([PDF](https://saeed.github.io/CS8803_DNS_Spring2024/assets/srd.pdf), extracted text). SRD sprays "packets over as many network paths as possible, while avoiding overloaded paths", provides "reliable but out-of-order delivery and leaves order restoration to the layers above", and retransmits in microseconds vs TCP's minimum RTO of "50 ms"; TCP was rejected because "latency outliers under congestion … can be anywhere between 50 ms and several seconds", and RoCE for "head-of-the-line blocking, congestion spreading, and occasional deadlocks" (same paper). In incast tests "SRD FCT [flow completion time] is close to the optimum with very low jitter" while TCP max FCT ran "3–20 times higher than the ideal" for 2 MB transfers.
- **Google Falcon**: "a reliable low-latency hardware transport" leveraging "Carousel, Snap, Swift, PLB, and CSIG", combining "fine-grained hardware-assisted round-trip time (RTT) measurements … per-flow hardware-enforced traffic shaping, and fast and accurate packet retransmissions", "first available in the Intel IPU E2000" — [Google Cloud blog announcement](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/systems/introducing-falcon-a-reliable-low-latency-hardware-transport), contributed to OCP in 2023 ([OCP Falcon spec repo](https://github.com/opencomputeproject/OCP-NET-Falcon), [Falcon spec v1.0](https://www.opencompute.org/documents/falcon-spec-v1-1-pdf-1)). Note the lineage: Snap/Pony Express (the software run-to-completion engine of §3.4) is explicitly listed as a technology Falcon hardens into silicon.
- **NVIDIA BlueField DPUs**: BlueField-3 is a "400Gb/s infrastructure compute platform" offloading "software-defined networking, storage, and cybersecurity" from host CPUs; BlueField-4 moves to 800 Gb/s ([NVIDIA DPU page](https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/networking/products/data-processing-unit/)).
- **How this compares to "write for one CPU"**: same logic, different substrate. Instead of compiling software for one CPU stepping (fragile, marginal gains), the hyperscalers built *one known NIC* deployed fleet-wide and moved the latency-critical state machine into it — eliminating exactly the costs the Homa ATC'21 paper says dominate in software (core hand-offs, wakeups, cache misses). Ousterhout's conclusion aligns: "Software implementations of transport protocols no longer make sense; those protocols need to move into the NICs", though "none of the existing 'smart NIC' architectures is adequate, he thinks" ([LWN part 2](https://lwn.net/Articles/914030/)).

## 5. Homa's own relationship to mechanical sympathy

### 5.1 The ATC'21 kernel paper: the protocol won; the multi-core software lost

Paper: [A Linux Kernel Implementation of the Homa Transport Protocol](https://www.usenix.org/conference/atc21/presentation/ousterhout), John Ousterhout, USENIX ATC'21 ([PDF](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/atc21-ousterhout.pdf); code: [PlatformLab/HomaModule](https://github.com/PlatformLab/HomaModule)). Hardware: 40-node CloudLab xl170 cluster, 10-core 2.4 GHz Xeons, Mellanox ConnectX-4 25 Gbps (PDF, extracted text).

Headline results (PDF, extracted text; corroborated by the [search-indexed abstract](https://www.usenix.org/conference/atc21/presentation/ousterhout)):

- "Homa/Linux provided lower latency than both TCP and DCTCP for all message sizes; for short messages, Homa's 99th percentile tail latency was 7–83x lower than TCP and DCTCP"; P50 short-message latency "3.5–7.5x lower than TCP and 2.7–3.8x lower than DCTCP"; best-case small-message RTT 15.1 µs (Homa) vs 23.4/24.1 µs (TCP/DCTCP).
- The [HomaModule README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/PlatformLab/HomaModule/master/README.md) claims, ongoing: "Homa's 99-th percentile latency is usually better than TCP's mean latency" and "tail latency is more than 10x better than TCP for all workloads I have measured."

The mechanical-sympathy findings — software overheads, not protocol, dominate (all PDF, extracted text from the [ATC'21 paper](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/atc21-ousterhout.pdf)):

- **Congestion moved from the network into the host**: "software congestion is becoming more problematic as more and more cores must be harnessed to keep up with increasing network speeds"; Homa "has eliminated network congestion as a significant factor", leaving software as "the primary factor limiting performance."
- **Single core can't keep up**: "a single core is too slow to handle all the protocol processing for a modern high-speed network", and "neither Homa nor TCP can utilize more than about one-third of the bandwidth of a 25 Gbps network for short-message workloads." At netdev, Ousterhout put it as: "A single core cannot handle more than 10Gbps but as soon as you split the job over multiple cores, there is an inherent performance penalty just for doing the split … you actually have to go to four cores before you actually get any performance improvement over one core" ([LWN part 2](https://lwn.net/Articles/914030/)).
- **Cross-core costs**: spreading a message's processing across cores slows each stage "by a factor of 2–3x (presumably due to cache coherency traffic)" — precisely the Disruptor's false-sharing/contention arithmetic, at kernel scale.
- **Load balancing (RSS/SoftIRQ placement) is the tail-latency villain**: "RSS … runs in NICs and distributes incoming packets across cores for NAPI processing using a hash of packet header fields"; "hot spots occur because the Linux scheduler's load balancer is unaware of the networking subsystem"; "hot spots remain the single greatest source of tail latency for Homa/Linux." (Corroborated: [HomaModule perf.txt](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/PlatformLab/HomaModule/master/perf.txt) logs "Intelligent SoftIRQ core selection produced 10-20% reduction in P99 latency" (2020) and "Gen3 load balancing reduced GRO→SoftIRQ handoff P99 from 71 µs to 8.7 µs" (2023).)
- **Thread wakeups**: "If a thread blocks in homa_recv, it takes about 2.5 µs to wake it on message arrival; this contributes significantly to latency."
- **Batching tradeoff (GRO)**: Homa repurposes GRO — it "defeats the GRO segregation and collect[s] all incoming packets into a single batch … allows Homa to batch more aggressively than TCP", but "the first packet in a batch must be delayed until the last is available. Larger batches can be processed more efficiently, but incur a larger latency penalty" — the Disruptor's throughput-vs-latency batching curve, restated for SoftIRQ.
- **Where the microseconds go**: for small messages, "total round-trip software overhead is about 9.5 µs" vs network time "about 5.6 µs per round-trip" — i.e., ~60%+ of the RTT is host software.
- **The pacer thread** (a very Disruptor-ish component — one dedicated thread owning all writes to the NIC queue to preserve run-time invariants): its job is to "limit the length of the NIC's internal transmit queue to enforce SRPT during output"; it "must poll the processor cycle counter to wait for the next transmission time" at "a time granularity of 1–2 µs", and "consumes most of a core under heavy output load." When it can't keep up, other cores assist ([micahlerner's paper summary](https://www.micahlerner.com/2021/08/29/a-linux-kernel-implementation-of-the-homa-transport-protocol.html) notes "it can not keep up at high bandwidth", with workarounds). Later field data: "Pacer does not prevent NIC queue buildup; 1ms+ periods with 500 KB queued" ([perf.txt](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/PlatformLab/HomaModule/master/perf.txt), Dec 2025 entry).
- **Upside estimate**: "Another factor of 5–10x in performance can be achieved if software overheads can be eliminated in the future"; "moving transport protocols to the NIC would reduce end-to-end application latency by at least 5x, increase small-message throughput by 5–10x."

### 5.2 The user-space DPDK Homa: same protocol, no kernel

Two distinct artifacts, often confused:

1. **The SIGCOMM'18 measurement implementation** — Homa inside RAMCloud's transport layer: "The Homa transport is based on DPDK, which allows it to bypass the kernel and communicate directly with the NIC; Homa detects incoming packets with polling rather than interrupts" ([Homa SIGCOMM'18, arXiv HTML](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1803.09615); [ACM DOI](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3230543.3230564); artifact: [PlatformLab/homa-paper-artifact](https://github.com/PlatformLab/homa-paper-artifact)). Numbers: "Our implementation of Homa delivers 99th percentile round-trip times less than 15 µs for short messages on a 10 Gbps network running at 80% load"; unloaded 100-byte echo RPC 4.7 µs. For contrast, an mTCP-based (DPDK user-level TCP) transport "was unable to achieve latencies … less than 1 ms" because "mTCP batches heavily" — batching tuned for throughput, not latency (same paper).
2. **[PlatformLab/Homa](https://github.com/PlatformLab/Homa)** — a standalone C++ userspace library: "a implementation of the Homa transport protocol that can be included by applications as a C++ library and can run completely in userspace, bypassing the kernel for the best possible performance", with "a DPDK based implementation of a Driver" (deps: CMake ≥3.11, DPDK 18.11). Status per its own README: "this repo is no longer under active development and the implementation is still at a relatively early stage", pointing to HomaModule as the production-ready path. **No published performance numbers for this specific library were found** (see gaps).

The same-protocol kernel-vs-bypass delta, in one line: **P99 ~14–15 µs (user space, DPDK, SIGCOMM'18 / RAMCloud) vs ~100 µs (Linux kernel module, ATC'21) for comparable short-message workloads** ([LWN part 2](https://lwn.net/Articles/914030/): "Stanford researchers implemented Homa in user space and were able to achieve 99th percentile round-trip latencies of 14µs, while the tail latency for Homa on Linux is 100µs"; ATC'21 PDF extracted text: "RAMCloud implementation of Homa provides P99 latency for W4 less than 15 µs, vs. about 100 µs for Homa/Linux"). The protocol is held constant; the ~7x tail gap is entirely the kernel's multi-core software structure — RSS hashing, GRO batching, SoftIRQ scheduling, wakeups, cache-coherency traffic.

### 5.3 Reading Homa through the LMAX lens

- Kernel Homa *cannot* follow the single-writer principle end-to-end: RSS scatters packets across cores by hash, SoftIRQ processing may run on yet another core, and the application thread on a third — three coherence-domain crossings per message where the Disruptor would have zero. The measured 2–3x per-stage slowdown from spreading ([ATC'21 PDF](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/atc21-ousterhout.pdf), extracted text) is the price.
- Homa's pacer is single-writer-per-resource applied to the NIC TX queue; its busy-poll at 1–2 µs granularity is a Disruptor busy-spin wait strategy; its GRO batching is the Disruptor's batch-drain effect — each borrowed under duress inside a framework (the kernel stack) built around the opposite assumptions.
- The user-space Homa implementations get to keep the whole recipe (poll, run-to-completion, per-core ownership, pre-allocation) — which is exactly where the missing ~7x tail latency lives.

## 6. Ousterhout directly on kernel vs bypass (and NICs)

- ATC'21 paper: identifies two escape routes from software overhead — moving transports to user space, or to the NIC — and quantifies the NIC option: "Moving transport protocols to the NIC would reduce end-to-end application latency by at least 5x, increase small-message throughput by 5–10x", with applications communicating "directly with the NIC using kernel bypass" ([ATC'21 PDF](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/atc21-ousterhout.pdf), extracted text; also surfaced by [micahlerner's summary](https://www.micahlerner.com/2021/08/29/a-linux-kernel-implementation-of-the-homa-transport-protocol.html): the two solutions are "moving transport protocols to user space" and "moving transport protocols to the NIC").
- ["It's Time to Replace TCP in the Datacenter" (arXiv 2210.00714)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.00714): "The best software protocol implementations have end-to-end latency more than 3x as high as implementations where applications communicate directly with the NIC via kernel bypass"; "software implementations give up a factor of 5–10x in small message throughput, compared with NIC-offloaded implementations"; "driving a 100 Gbps network at 80% utilization in both directions consumes 10–20 cores just in the networking stack"; "load balancing overheads are now one of the primary sources of tail latency" ([full text via ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2210.00714)).
- Netdev 0x16 keynote ([conference page](https://netdevconf.info/0x16/sessions/keynote/keynote-ousterhout.html), [video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=o2HBHckrdQc), [LWN part 1](https://lwn.net/Articles/913260/), [LWN part 2](https://lwn.net/Articles/914030/)):
  - "Network speeds are increasing at this amazing rate, CPU speeds aren't changing much. Software simply cannot keep up and he sees no solution to that problem."
  - "Software implementations of transport protocols no longer make sense; those protocols need to move into the NICs." And: "None of the existing 'smart NIC' architectures is adequate, he thinks, so it makes for an interesting problem for computer architects to solve."
  - His cache-coherency diagnosis of the multi-core penalty: "cache-coherency traffic coming from the split reduces the performance when compared to doing the processing on a single core" ([LWN part 2](https://lwn.net/Articles/914030/)) — Ousterhout independently lands on Thompson's "managing cache misses is the single largest limitation" ([Single Writer Principle](https://mechanical-sympathy.blogspot.com/2011/09/single-writer-principle.html)).
  - Notably, he built the kernel module anyway — "runs on Linux 5.17 and 5.18 and is not meant as a 'research prototype'" ([LWN part 2](https://lwn.net/Articles/914030/)) — because deployability inside the kernel ecosystem matters for adoption, even at a known ~7x tail-latency cost vs bypass.

## 7. Synthesis (for the eventual write-up)

1. **The Disruptor's numbers are cache-coherence numbers** (52 ns mean inter-thread handoff, ~26M ops/s single-producer — [technical paper](https://lmax-exchange.github.io/disruptor/disruptor.html)); the datacenter floor is ~2 µs bypass / ~15–25 µs kernel RTT ([CACM](https://cacm.acm.org/research/attack-of-the-killer-microseconds/), [eRPC](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.00680), [ATC'21](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/atc21-ousterhout.pdf)). Numbers don't transfer; the cost model does.
2. **The design rules transferred wholesale** into the run-to-completion school: eRPC (exclusive per-thread endpoints, polling, common-case optimisation), Seastar (shared-nothing shards), DPDK (per-lcore lockless queues, burst amortisation), Snap (single-threaded spinning engines over lock-free mmap'd rings), io_uring (SPSC rings + barriers between user and kernel), Shenango/Caladan (making core-dedication affordable), Demikernel (ns-budget datapath OS).
3. **Nobody ships per-CPU-model transports**; they ship microarchitecture-aware code (padding, NUMA, DDIO, aRFS) plus optional `-march=native` builds. The genuine "homogeneous special hardware" strategy migrated to fleet-wide custom NICs: SRD-in-Nitro, Falcon-in-IPU, BlueField.
4. **Homa is the natural experiment**: one protocol, two implementations — kernel (structurally forbidden from single-writer/run-to-completion by RSS/GRO/SoftIRQ/wakeups; P99 ~100 µs) vs user-space DPDK (full recipe available; P99 ~14–15 µs). The ~7x gap is the empirical price of ignoring mechanical sympathy at the host, and Ousterhout's stated endgame is to move the whole state machine into the NIC for another 5–10x.

## 8. Gaps — things I could NOT verify

1. **USENIX and ACM pages returned HTTP 403** to direct fetches; quotes from the [Homa ATC'21 PDF](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/atc21-ousterhout.pdf), [Snap SOSP'19 PDF](https://courses.grainger.illinois.edu/cs598hpn/fa2020/papers/snap.pdf), [SRD IEEE Micro PDF](https://saeed.github.io/CS8803_DNS_Spring2024/assets/srd.pdf), [DDIO ATC'20 PDF](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/atc20-farshin.pdf) and [CACM Killer Microseconds](https://cacm.acm.org/research/attack-of-the-killer-microseconds/) were obtained via a text-extraction proxy (marked "PDF, extracted text"). Key numbers were cross-checked against independent secondary sources (LWN, acolyer, perf.txt, search-indexed abstracts) and are consistent, but **exact quote wording should be re-verified against the PDFs before quoting verbatim in any published doc**.
2. **No published performance numbers for the standalone [PlatformLab/Homa](https://github.com/PlatformLab/Homa) C++/DPDK library** were found — no dedicated "Homa/DPDK paper" appears to exist. The closest published user-space numbers are the SIGCOMM'18 RAMCloud+DPDK implementation (P99 <15 µs at 80% load, 10 Gbps). The repo self-describes as early-stage and no longer actively developed.
3. **Snap "38 µs" figure**: could not verify any 38-microsecond latency number; sources give "less than 10 µs" spin-polling latency and a *38 Gbps* (on 1.05 cores) throughput figure — possibly the origin of a confused "38" citation elsewhere.
4. **Disruptor "<50 ns" vs published 52 ns**: the site headline ("latencies lower than 50 nanoseconds") is not exactly reproduced by the paper's own Table 4 (mean 52 ns); treated as ≈50 ns, different runs/hardware. No independent third-party replication of the 25M msgs/s figure was checked.
5. **Michael Barker = `mikeb01`**: inferred from the GitHub contributor listing plus his LMAX blog author page and SE Radio appearance; GitHub's API does not return real names, so this is high-confidence inference, not a primary-source statement.
6. **MicroQuanta specifics**: got the mechanism quote ("runs for a configurable runtime out of every period") but not the concrete default runtime/period values used in production.
7. **"Nobody ships transports compiled for one specific CPU model"** is an absence claim — inherently unverifiable exhaustively. I found no counterexample; DPDK/ScyllaDB evidence shows the industry pattern is generic binaries + runtime tuning. Proprietary HFT stacks (which do tune per-machine) publish nothing citable and are out of datacenter-transport scope.
8. **[kernel.dk/io_uring.pdf](https://kernel.dk/io_uring.pdf)** (Axboe's "Efficient IO with io_uring") could not be fetched (TLS certificate mismatch on the host, then proxy 422); memory-barrier specifics for io_uring are cited from the [LWN article](https://lwn.net/Articles/776703/) instead. Similarly, Shenango's "every 5 µs" figure comes from the paper's abstract as reflected in search results and the [paper PDF link](http://www.abelay.me/data/shenango_nsdi19.pdf), not from a direct read.
