## 1. Does Meta have a private datacenter transport? Answer: No general-purpose one. TCP on the frontend, RoCEv2 + CTran/NCCLX on the AI backend, QUIC (mvfst) at the edge.

- Meta's SIGCOMM 2024 paper ["RDMA over Ethernet for Distributed AI Training at Meta Scale"](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3651890.3672233) (Aug 2024; [PDF](https://engineering.fb.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/sigcomm24-final246.pdf), [blog version](https://engineering.fb.com/2024/08/05/data-center-engineering/roce-network-distributed-ai-training-at-scale/)) — verified real. Authors: Gangidi, Miao, Zheng, et al. (Meta). Meta chose **commodity RoCEv2** for the AI *backend* fabric ("encapsulates the RDMA service in UDP packets"); the *frontend* (FE) network (data ingestion, checkpointing, logging, storage) is the classic RSW/FSW Ethernet fabric.
- **"TCP remains the primary network transport protocol that supports thousands of services on top of it"** — Meta's own framing of its frontend datacenter network (per search-result synthesis of Meta engineering material around the [RoCE blog post](https://engineering.fb.com/2024/08/05/data-center-engineering/roce-network-distributed-ai-training-at-scale/), Aug 2024). **[Wording UNVERIFIED against a single primary page — research cut short; the substance (frontend = TCP, backend = RoCEv2) is verified across the SIGCOMM24 paper and OCP 2025 blog]**
- Oct 2025: Meta open-sourced **CTran**, a transport-layer backend beneath its **NCCLX** collective-communications library, scaling AI training to >100K GPUs; supports NVLink, InfiniBand, RoCE, and TCP ([SDxCentral, 2025-10-24](https://www.sdxcentral.com/news/meta-open-sources-transport-stack-to-scale-ai-training-to-over-100k-gpus/)). This is a **userspace AI-collectives transport**, not a general RPC transport.
- [OCP Summit 2025 blog (2025-10-13)](https://engineering.fb.com/2025/10/13/data-infrastructure/ocp-summit-2025-the-open-future-of-networking-hardware-for-ai/): Meta's networking hardware roadmap — Minipack3N (NVIDIA Spectrum-4 51T switch), **DSF (Disaggregated Scheduled Fabric)** scaling to 18,432 XPUs with "an open and standard Ethernet-based RoCE interface". All fabric/hardware innovation; no new software transport.
- Edge/user-facing transport: Meta built **mvfst** (its QUIC implementation) and moved most user traffic to QUIC ([How Facebook is bringing QUIC to billions, 2020-10-21](https://engineering.fb.com/2020/10/21/networking-traffic/how-facebook-is-bringing-quic-to-billions/); [QUIC+TCP innovations talk, 2022-07-06](https://engineering.fb.com/2022/07/06/networking-traffic/watch-metas-engineers-discuss-quic-and-tcp-innovations-for-our-network/)). Notably the [mvfst README](https://github.com/facebook/mvfst) states the goal is a QUIC implementation "for use cases on both the internet and the **data-center**" — i.e., Meta's in-datacenter transport experimentation vehicle appears to be userspace QUIC, not a kernel TCP replacement. (Actual internal DC QUIC deployment: **[UNVERIFIED]**.)
- **FBNIC**: Meta built its own NIC + upstream driver (`drivers/net/ethernet/meta/fbnic`), posted by Alexander Duyck in April 2024 ([LWN, 2024-04](https://lwn.net/Articles/969383/); [driver merge article](https://lwn.net/Articles/980445/); [Netdev 0x18 talk](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FOoK1vL0UHQ)). Meta's host-networking investment goes into **owning the NIC hardware under standard protocols**, not replacing the protocol.
- Google contributed its **Falcon** hardware transport to OCP in 2023 ([Google Cloud blog](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/systems/introducing-falcon-a-reliable-low-latency-hardware-transport); [OCP spec v1.0](https://www.opencompute.org/documents/falcon-spec-v1-1-pdf-1)). One report states Falcon was "developed by Google with the help of hyperscalers Meta Platforms and Microsoft as well as Nvidia" ([Converge Digest](https://convergedigest.com/ocp-summit-google-opens-falcon-for-hardware-transport/) — **[UNVERIFIED: page returned 403 before the Meta-involvement sentence could be confirmed]**).

## 3. Meta's latency reality: they dodged TCP on the hottest path in 2013, in userspace

- ["Scaling Memcache at Facebook" (NSDI 2013)](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/conference/nsdi13/nsdi13-final170_update.pdf) — verified: **get requests use UDP** (bypassing TCP connection state/latency on the hottest path); get errors are treated as cache misses. **Incast is handled by a client-side sliding window** limiting in-flight requests, plus per-thread UDP ports ([paper](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/conference/nsdi13/nsdi13-final170_update.pdf); [summary](https://timilearning.com/posts/mit-6.824/lecture-16-memcache-at-facebook/)).
- Verified latency numbers from the same paper: **median memcache request latency 333 µs; p75 = 475 µs; p95 = 1.135 ms** over 7 days ([paper](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/conference/nsdi13/nsdi13-final170_update.pdf); corroborated in [Hemant Gupta's paper notes](https://hemantkgupta.medium.com/insights-from-paper-scaling-memcache-at-facebook-1386a5956859)). At sub-ms end-to-end with client-side mitigation already in place, the µs-scale transport wins Homa offers are a second-order term for this stack.
- Homa's own benchmark workloads are (partly) Facebook's — **verified verbatim from Table 1 of the [SIGCOMM 2018 Homa paper PDF](https://people.csail.mit.edu/alizadeh/papers/homa-sigcomm18.pdf)** (extracted from the PDF text streams, 2026-07-23): W1 = "Accesses to a collection of memcached servers at Facebook, as approximated by the statistical model of the ETC workload"; W3 = "Aggregated workload from all applications running in a Google datacenter"; W4 = "Hadoop cluster at Facebook"; W5 = "Web search workload used for DCTCP". So Meta's workloads motivated Homa — **Meta's data, not Meta's engineers**.
- RPC layer: Meta's service mesh **ServiceRouter** routes "tens of billions of requests per second across tens of thousands of services" via a linked-in routing library for 99% of RPCs ([OSDI 2023](https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi23/presentation/saokar); [slides](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~dskarlat/slides/sr_osdi23_slides.pdf)) — Meta's published tail-latency work optimizes routing/locality ("locality rings"), not the transport.
- Microburst reality: Meta runs **Millisampler** fleet-wide for ms/µs-granularity traffic characterization ([At Scale talk](https://atscaleconference.com/videos/millisampler-fine-grained-network-traffic-analysis/); [IMC 2022 paper "A microscopic view of bursts, buffer contention, and loss in data centers"](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3517745.3561430)) — Meta measures exactly the incast/burst phenomena Homa targets, and responds with buffer/fabric/host tuning rather than a new transport.

I.e., where Meta *does* want a post-TCP transport (AI/HPC), it is betting on **standards-body hardware transports**, not a kernel software protocol.
- **Kernel host networking**: FBNIC driver (2024, above), io_uring zcrx (merged 6.15, above). Meta remains the outlier that stayed on commodity TCP/RoCE + open standards.

## 5. Alternative explanations from Meta's side (why not fund Homa)

- **Application-layer tail mitigation already deployed**: UDP gets + sliding-window incast control since 2013 (NSDI13, above); mcrouter/ServiceRouter load-balancing and locality rings (OSDI23, above).
- **Utilization is managed at the fleet level, not the transport level**: **Twine** cluster manager runs "nearly all" Meta services on millions of machines with elastic capacity shifting, entitlements, autoscaling (SRM), stacking ([OSDI 2020 paper](https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi20/presentation/tang); [Meta blog 2020-11-11](https://engineering.fb.com/2020/11/11/data-center-engineering/twine-2/)).
- **Kraken** (OSDI 2016): live-traffic load testing "improved hardware utilization by over 20%", with a stated **93% peak-utilization target** in cluster load tests ([USENIX](https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi16/technical-sessions/presentation/veeraraghavan); [Morning Paper summary](https://blog.acolyer.org/2016/11/28/kraken-leveraging-live-traffic-tests-to-identify-and-resolve-resource-utilization-bottlenecks-in-large-scale-web-services/)). This supports the hypothesis that Meta's headroom is *managed and already squeezed* by fleet tooling; remaining headroom is failure/burst insurance.
- **Headroom as disaster insurance**: Meta runs mandatory region-drain drills (Belljar tooling; multi-hour full-region drain tests across its ~13 regions) to guarantee survival of region loss ([At Scale: "(Almost) Fail & Tell: Stop the World"](https://atscaleconference.com/almost-fail-tell-stop-the-world/)). Capacity reserved to absorb a region failure cannot be reclaimed by a faster transport — consistent with the "no transport can buy back DR headroom" hypothesis.

## Bottom line (assessment)

Meta has **not** publicly evaluated, adopted, or even commented on Homa — anywhere, including from its own netdev co-maintainer, whose 11 Homa-thread messages are purely administrative. This is best read not as an oversight but as revealed strategy: Meta already de-fanged its worst TCP tail problem in userspace in 2013 (UDP gets + incast windowing), buys utilization with fleet tooling (Twine/Kraken, 93% load-test targets) while holding headroom as region-failure insurance, and directs all transport-level investment at the AI backend via standards bodies (UEC steering seat, RoCEv2, DSF, FBNIC, CTran). Meanwhile Homa's Linux upstreaming is starving: v19 unreviewed by humans for ~3 months as of 2026-07-23, with only Red Hat (Abeni) ever providing sustained review and Alibaba providing the only corporate line-by-line scrutiny.
