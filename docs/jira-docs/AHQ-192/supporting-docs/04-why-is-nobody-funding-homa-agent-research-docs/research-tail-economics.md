## Verdict up front

The claimed causal chain is: *Homa fixes P99 transport latency under load → operators can run latency-sensitive fabrics hot instead of over-provisioning → capex/opex savings proportional to fleet size → $100M+/yr at hyperscale.*

| Link in the chain | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Homa's P99 small-message win over TCP is real | **Holds** (in testbeds) | 7–83x P99 reduction measured, but on 40-node clusters, not at operator scale |
| Transport is a major share of end-to-end P99 in typical services | **Mostly fails** | Published tail-cause taxonomies name host/app causes; transport µs vs service ms |
| The remaining tail win isn't already harvested in software | **Fails** | Hedging alone: 99.9th 1,800ms → 74ms for +2% load (Google BigTable) |
| Fabrics run at 30–40% *because of transport tail fear* | **Fails** | Measured fabrics run at ~1–25% for burst/failure/growth reasons; microbursts, not average-rate fear, dominate |
| Network is a big enough cost slice for the saving | **Weak** | Network ≈ 8–15% of DC TCO; and Homa doesn't reduce the 45–57% server slice |
| The µs-critical niche is unserved | **Fails** | Azure: ~70% of all traffic already RDMA; Google Snap/Falcon; AWS SRD; FB memcached-UDP |
| "$100M+/yr" magnitude | **Not absurd, but mechanism-less** | $100M ≈ 0.3% of Meta-scale annual capex — small % wins DO clear $100M at hyperscale, but the specific Homa mechanism doesn't produce them; the wins that do (CPU-core savings) come from *offload*, which kernel Homa doesn't provide |

**Bottom line:** Homa's latency numbers are genuine, but the $100M argument transplants a testbed transport win into an economic slot that operators have already filled three other ways: software tail-tolerance, point-solution transports (RDMA/SRD/Falcon/UDP), and cheap bandwidth. Where the money is real (fleet CPU efficiency), kernel-space Homa doesn't deliver it — hardware offload does, and that's what hyperscalers actually built.

## 0. What Homa actually demonstrated (baseline for the whole argument)

- **[V]** Homa (SIGCOMM 2018, Montazeri, Li, Alizadeh, Ousterhout): on a 10 Gbps testbed at **80% network load**, 99th-percentile RTT **< 15 µs** for short messages; P99 "slowdown" (observed time ÷ unloaded best case) for the shortest 50% of messages **never worse than 2.2x**. Receiver-driven scheduling + switch priority queues + controlled overcommitment. Sources: [Homa SIGCOMM'18 PDF](https://people.csail.mit.edu/alizadeh/papers/homa-sigcomm18.pdf), [arXiv:1803.09615](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09615).
- **[V]** Homa/Linux kernel module (ATC 2021, Ousterhout): **P99 latency 7–83x lower than TCP and DCTCP for short messages under high load** (this is presumably where the prompt's "10–72x" figure family comes from; the ATC'21 range is 7–83x). Critically, the same paper reports that **"both tail latency and throughput are now limited by software overheads"** (load-imbalance of the stack across cores) and that **"another factor of 5–10x"** is available only **if software overheads can be eliminated**. Source: [ATC21 paper PDF](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/atc21-ousterhout.pdf), [talk page](https://www.usenix.org/conference/atc21/presentation/ousterhout).
- **[V]** Ousterhout's position paper "It's Time to Replace TCP in the Datacenter" (2022): on a 40-node, 25 Gbps cluster at 80% utilization, TCP short messages suffer ~**10x** worse slowdown than long ones; DCTCP still ~**3x**. Also: driving **100 Gbps at 80% utilization both directions costs 10–20 CPU cores** just in the (TCP) networking stack; software transports vs NIC-offloaded ones: **>3x** end-to-end latency, **5–10x** lower small-message throughput. He concedes: "It is hard to imagine a computing standard more entrenched than TCP." Source: [arXiv:2210.00714](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.00714).
- **[V]** Published critique: Ivan Pepelnjak's analysis argues the headline TCP-vs-Homa comparisons are inflated by the benchmark multiplexing many independent messages over few TCP connections, manufacturing transport-level head-of-line blocking that a well-designed RPC layer (connection pools) avoids. Source: [ipSpace: Is It Time to Replace TCP in Data Centers?](https://blog.ipspace.net/2023/01/data-center-tcp-replacement/); see also [LWN: Moving past TCP in the data center](https://lwn.net/Articles/913260/) and [Geoff Huston, APNIC: Death of TCP predicted: News at 11](https://blog.apnic.net/2023/05/22/death-of-tcp-predicted-news-at-11/).
- **[M]** Deployment status: no publicly documented production deployment of Homa at any major operator as of mid-2026; the [PlatformLab/HomaModule](https://github.com/PlatformLab/HomaModule) kernel module remains a research artifact with on-and-off Linux upstreaming discussion ([LWN 2022 coverage](https://lwn.net/Articles/913260/)).

**Scale caveat (reasoned inference, not measurement):** all Homa evidence is from ≤40–100 node single-fabric testbeds/simulations with synthetic workload mixes (W1–W5). There is no published operator-scale (10k+ node, multi-tenant, failure-rich) validation of "tails stay flat at 80–90% load."

## 1. What fraction of end-to-end tail latency is network *transport*?

### 1.1 The canonical tail-cause taxonomies barely mention transport

- **[V]** Dean & Barroso, **"The Tail at Scale" (CACM, Feb 2013)** name as causes of latency variability: **shared resources (CPU contention from other apps), daemons/background jobs, global resource sharing (network switches, shared file systems), maintenance activities (log compaction, garbage collection), queueing at multiple levels, power limits/CPU throttling, SSD garbage collection, and energy-management (power-save modes)**. The network appears as *one queueing locus among many*, not as the headline cause. Source: [The Tail at Scale PDF (barroso.org)](https://www.barroso.org/publications/TheTailAtScale.pdf). *(Cause list paraphrased from the paper; the CACM full-text fetch 403'd this session, so quotes could not be re-checked verbatim — see Gaps.)*
- **[V]** The famous fan-out amplification math from the same paper: a server with 10 ms typical / 1 s P99 latency → one request in 100 is slow; fan out to **100 such servers in parallel and 63% of user requests take >1 s**. Note what this says: fan-out amplifies *whatever* per-leaf tail exists — GC pauses amplify exactly as much as transport queueing does. Fan-out math is transport-agnostic. Source: [The Tail at Scale PDF](https://www.barroso.org/publications/TheTailAtScale.pdf).
- **[V]** **"Tales of the Tail" (SoCC 2014**, Li, Sharma, Ports, Gribble): measured tail causes for a null-RPC server, memcached, and nginx: **interference from background processes, non-FIFO request scheduling, suboptimal interrupt routing, CPU power-saving (C-states), NUMA effects, constrained concurrency models**. After systematically eliminating these *host-level* factors, **memcached achieved 11 µs median and 32 µs 99.9th-percentile latency at 80% utilization** on a 4-core box — i.e., with the host fixed, tails approached the queueing-theoretic ideal *over ordinary kernel TCP/UDP networking*. Transport was not among their dominant causes. Sources: [paper PDF](https://drkp.net/papers/latency-socc14.pdf), [ACM DOI](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2670979.2670988).

### 1.2 The strongest pro-network data point — and why it's not what it looks like

- **[V]** **DeathStarBench (ASPLOS 2019**, Gan, Delimitrou et al.): in microservice decompositions, **"network processing" rises to 36.3% of total execution time** (vs a small share in the equivalent monolith); network processing accounts for **>30% of tail latency in the Swarm/IoT settings even at low load**; communication is **≥ one third of median and tail end-to-end latency** in the studied apps (lower in e-commerce/banking, which are compute-heavier). Sources: [ASPLOS'19 paper (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/332218924_An_Open-Source_Benchmark_Suite_for_Microservices_and_Their_Hardware-Software_Implications_for_Cloud_Edge_Systems), [canonical PDF, Cornell **[M]**](https://www.csl.cornell.edu/~delimitrou/papers/2019.asplos.microservices.pdf).
- **Key caveat (reasoned inference):** DeathStarBench's "network processing" is overwhelmingly **host-side CPU time in the kernel stack and RPC libraries** (syscalls, copies, protobuf/Thrift, TCP processing) — not in-fabric queueing delay. Homa-as-kernel-module attacks fabric queueing and protocol behavior but **still pays the kernel software costs**; the ATC'21 Homa paper itself says software overheads now dominate **[V]**. The DeathStarBench number is better evidence for *kernel-bypass/offload/RPC-acceleration* (e.g., the [Dagger NIC-offload work](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2007.08622) built on the same benchmark) than for a new kernel transport.
- **[V]** Same story at hyperscale, from the CPU side: Google's fleet profiling (**Kanev et al., ISCA 2015**, 20,000 machines over 3 years) found the **"datacenter tax" — protobuf handling, RPC, hashing, compression, memory allocation/movement — is ~22–27%, approaching 30% of all fleet cycles**; within the RPC tax, ~1/3 is balancing/encryption/failure-detection and the rest is *payload data movement*. Source: [Profiling a Warehouse-Scale Computer PDF](https://static.googleusercontent.com/media/research.google.com/en//pubs/archive/44271.pdf).
- **[V]** **Meta (Accelerometer, ASPLOS 2020**, Sriraman & Dhanotia): Facebook's top-7 microservices spend **as little as 18% of CPU cycles on core application logic**; the rest is orchestration — I/O processing, (de)serialization, logging, compression. Sources: [paper PDF](https://akshithasriraman.eecs.umich.edu/publication/asplos/accelerometer.pdf), [Meta engineering blog](https://engineering.fb.com/2020/05/11/data-center-engineering/accelerometer-and-softsku/).

### 1.3 The magnitude mismatch (the µs-vs-ms argument)

Reasoned quantitative framing (inference from the verified numbers above, not a single citable measurement):

- A user-facing service P99 of **50–200 ms** is typical (Dean & Barroso discuss interactive budgets of ~100 ms **[V-paper context]**).
- Unloaded kernel-TCP RTT in a modern fabric is ~**25–50 µs**; Homa's improvement window — the gap between TCP's loaded P99 for small messages (hundreds of µs to a few ms under incast/congestion) and Homa's ~15 µs — is **at most a few ms, usually a few hundred µs, per network hop-pair**.
- Even with Dean-style fan-out amplification of, say, 5 serial RPC stages: **~1–10 ms** of recoverable end-to-end tail in a bad case, against 50–200 ms budgets dominated by app-level causes (GC, thread pools, cold caches, interference — the causes §1.1 sources actually name). **Fixing transport moves the needle materially only where the whole request budget is itself µs–low-ms scale** — cache/KV tiers, storage metadata, search leaf fan-out, disaggregated memory — which is exactly the niche §5 shows is already served.
- **No published study was found that attributes X% of end-to-end P99 to the transport layer for a production estate** — this attribution simply doesn't appear in the literature we could reach; operators publish cause taxonomies (§1.1) that put host/app effects first. (See Gaps.)

## 2. Software tail mitigations already deployed (the "already harvested" point)

- **[V]** **Hedged requests** (Dean & Barroso 2013): in a real Google BigTable benchmark reading 1,000 keys spread over 100 tablet servers, sending a hedge after a 10 ms defer **cut 99.9th-percentile latency from 1,800 ms to 74 ms** — a **24x** tail reduction — **while sending only ~2% more requests**. Sources: [The Tail at Scale PDF](https://www.barroso.org/publications/TheTailAtScale.pdf), [secondary write-up](https://dev.to/gabrielanhaia/request-hedging-the-tail-at-scale-technique-most-teams-skip-2j37).
- **[V-paper context]** The same paper describes **tied requests** (enqueue on two servers, first-to-start cancels the other), micro-partitioning, selective replication, and latency-induced probation — all application/RPC-layer, all deployed at Google in 2013, all transport-agnostic.
- **[M]** These techniques are now commodity, available off-the-shelf: [gRPC request hedging](https://grpc.io/docs/guides/request-hedging/) (config-level feature), [Envoy request hedging](https://www.envoyproxy.io/docs/envoy/latest/intro/arch_overview/http/http_routing#request-hedging), [MongoDB hedged reads](https://www.mongodb.com/docs/manual/core/read-preference-hedge-option/), [Cassandra speculative retry / rapid read protection](https://www.datastax.com/blog/rapid-read-protection-cassandra-204) (2013). None were re-verified live this session.
- **Implication (inference):** the cheap 10–25x tail-at-the-99.9th wins that Homa's pitch implicitly monetizes were largely captured a decade ago at the application layer for ~2% extra load, with zero fabric or kernel changes. Homa's *incremental* value is the residual tail hedging can't fix (correlated slowness under true congestion/incast) — real, but far smaller than the headline "72x vs TCP".

## 3. Do fabrics actually run at 30–40% because of tail fear? (No.)

- **[V]** **Benson, Akella, Maltz, "Network Traffic Characteristics of Data Centers in the Wild" (IMC 2010**, 10 DCs: university, enterprise, cloud): utilization is **highest in the core, yet at any moment <25% of core links are "heavily utilized"**; ≥80% of edge links show <10% utilization variation; **losses concentrate near the edge**, driven by bursts, not sustained load. Source: [IMC'10 paper PDF](https://conferences.sigcomm.org/imc/2010/papers/p267.pdf).
- **[V]** **Facebook, "Inside the Social Network's (Datacenter) Network" (SIGCOMM 2015**, Roy et al.): **"link utilization is on the order of 1% most of the time"**, yet **over two-thirds of the shared switch buffer is utilized within each 10 µs interval** — i.e., the constraint that bites is **µs-scale microbursts against buffer depth, not average bandwidth**. Traffic is "widespread, uniform and stable, with rapidly changing, internally bursty heavy hitters." Source: [FB SIGCOMM'15 paper PDF](https://cseweb.ucsd.edu/~snoeren/papers/fb-sigcomm15.pdf); corroborating [µs-granularity microburst measurements, IMC 2017](https://conferences.sigcomm.org/imc/2017/papers/imc17-final60.pdf).
- **[V]** **Google Jupiter**: fabric bisection grew 1.3 Pb/s (2015) → >6 Pb/s (2022) → **13.1 Pb/s at 400 Gb/s links** (2023), with a claimed **50x reliability improvement** — capacity growth and availability, not utilization-raising, are what Google brags about. Source: [Google Cloud blog, 25 years of data center networking](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/networking/speed-scale-reliability-25-years-of-data-center-networking). The [Jupiter Evolving SIGCOMM'22 paper](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3544216.3544265) reports traffic/topology engineering in terms of *Max Link Utilization* management (99th-pct MLU within 15% of optimal) **[V-partial, via search snippet]**, and claims (from its abstract) **~5x capacity/speed increase, 30% capex reduction, 41% power reduction** from the OCS-based evolution **[M — abstract figures recalled, not re-fetched; verify before quoting]**.
- **Why fabrics carry headroom (synthesis of the above, inference):** (a) **burst absorption** at µs timescales — FB's 1%-average-but-buffers-full data shows average utilization could not be pushed to 80% without microburst loss regardless of transport; (b) **failure headroom** — Clos fabrics must absorb the loss of switch planes/links without SLO breach; (c) **growth provisioning** — fabrics are built years ahead of demand (Jupiter's whole narrative); (d) oversubscription economics already dial in deliberate ratios. **None of these constraints is removed by a transport protocol.** Homa flattening P99-vs-load curves addresses a fifth, smaller factor (congestion-tail fear) that the published record does not identify as the binding constraint.
- **Counterpoint for honesty [M]:** Google's private WAN B4 famously runs links at **near 100% utilization** via centralized TE ([B4, SIGCOMM 2013](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2486001.2486019)) — proving utilization is a *traffic-engineering and loss-tolerance* decision. Where traffic is elastic and delay-tolerant, operators already run hot **without** a new transport; where it's latency-critical, headroom exists for burst/failure reasons a transport can't waive.

## 4. Cost structure: what slice could Homa even touch?

- **[V]** **Greenberg et al., "The Cost of a Cloud" (SIGCOMM CCR 2009)** — amortized monthly DC cost: **servers ~45%, power distribution & cooling infrastructure ~25%, power draw ~15%, network ~15%** (50,000 servers, $3,000/server, 5% cost of money, 3-yr server amortization → $52.5M/yr server cost in their model). Source: [paper PDF (MSR)](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2009/01/p68-v39n1o-greenberg.pdf).
- **[V]** **James Hamilton (AWS), "Overall Data Center Costs" (2010)**: **servers & storage 57%, networking gear 8%, power distribution & cooling 18%, power 13%, other 4%** (46k servers, 8 MW critical load, $1,450/server @165 W, 3-yr server / 5-yr network / 10-yr facility amortization, PUE 1.45). Hamilton's later commentary: even in GPU-heavy DCs with 2-orders-of-magnitude more bandwidth per host, **network is only ~15% of all-in cost**. Source: [Perspectives blog post](https://perspectives.mvdirona.com/2010/09/overall-data-center-costs/).
- So the network slice is **~8–15% of TCO**, and has stayed in that band from 2009 CPU-era to GPU-era estimates. **A transport protocol can only harvest savings from that slice** (by deferring bandwidth/switch purchases) **or from the server slice via CPU savings** — and §0 shows kernel Homa does not save host CPU (its own authors report software overheads dominate; TCP's 10–20 cores per loaded 100 Gbps NIC problem is a *kernel-stack* problem Homa-in-kernel shares, and the paper's stated escape hatch is offload — which is RDMA/Falcon/SRD territory, §5).
- **The "$100M/yr" arithmetic sanity check [M for capex figures]:** Meta's 2024 capex was ~**$39B**, with 2025 guidance raised into the **$64–72B** range (AI build-out) — see [Meta 10-K/investor materials](https://investor.atmeta.com/financials/). Alphabet guided ~**$85B** for 2025 ([Q2'25 earnings call coverage](https://abc.xyz/investor/)). Against that, **$100M/yr ≈ 0.15–0.3% of one company's annual infrastructure capex** — so the *magnitude* of the claim is not inherently silly; hyperscalers fund hundred-engineer teams (Snap, Falcon, SRD) precisely because sub-1% fleet efficiencies clear $100M. **What fails is the mechanism**: the specific savings channel claimed for Homa (run the general fabric at 80–90% instead of over-provisioning) contradicts §3, and the channel that demonstrably pays (fleet CPU) is served by offload transports, not kernel Homa.
- **"Facebook rents out a third of its servers" (Steve's hypothetical):** No transport change can do this. Server counts are provisioned on **application CPU/DRAM demand at peak + redundancy**, not on transport latency. Fleet studies show chronic *server* underutilization for scheduling/peak reasons: Google Borg cells averaged roughly **25–35% CPU / ~40% memory allocation-vs-use** ([Borg, EuroSys 2015](https://research.google/pubs/large-scale-cluster-management-at-google-with-borg/)) **[M]**; Azure found most VMs average **<20% CPU** ([Resource Central, SOSP 2017](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/resource-central-understanding-and-predicting-workloads-for-improved-resource-management-in-large-cloud-platforms/)) **[M]**. The headroom exists for demand-peak/failure reasons that Homa does not address. The *only* transport-adjacent server saving with published evidence is **CPU-core reclamation via offload**: Azure explicitly motivates its RDMA deployment by "significant CPU core savings" from bypassing the kernel TCP stack ([NSDI 2023](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/nsdi23-bai.pdf)) **[V]**, and Google Snap reports **3x better Gbps/core than kernel TCP** ([SOSP 2019](https://courses.grainger.illinois.edu/ece598hpn/fa2022/papers/snap.pdf)) **[V]**. Kernel Homa provides neither.

## 5. The µs-critical niche is already served by point solutions

Where transport µs genuinely dominate the request budget, hyperscalers deployed purpose-built transports years ago:

- **[V]** **Microsoft Azure**: **~70% of ALL Azure traffic is already RDMA** (intra-region RDMA enabled in all public regions), deployed for storage frontend+backend, motivated by disk I/O latency *and CPU core savings*. Source: [Empowering Azure Storage with RDMA, NSDI 2023](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/nsdi23-bai.pdf). (Predecessor: [RDMA over commodity Ethernet at scale, SIGCOMM 2016](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2934872.2934908) **[M]**.)
- **[V]** **Google**: **Snap/Pony Express** (SOSP 2019) — userspace microkernel networking, **sub-10 µs latency, 3x Gbps/core vs kernel, 5M remote-memory-ops/s on one core (2x the hardware RDMA it replaced)**, in production ≥3 years by 2019. Sources: [Illinois course copy of the paper](https://courses.grainger.illinois.edu/ece598hpn/fa2022/papers/snap.pdf), [The Morning Paper summary](https://blog.acolyer.org/2019/11/11/snap-networking/). Successor: **Falcon**, Google's *hardware* reliable low-latency transport (opened via OCP 2023), explicitly layering production-proven Swift congestion control + PLB + CSIG into NIC hardware. Sources: [Google Cloud blog](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/systems/introducing-falcon-a-reliable-low-latency-hardware-transport), [Falcon SIGCOMM 2025 paper](https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.1145/3718958.3754353).
- **[M]** **AWS**: **SRD (Scalable Reliable Datagram)** in the Nitro NIC — multipath spraying, out-of-order delivery, ~**85% lower P99.9** vs TCP on EBS io2 Block Express; rolled out under EFA (HPC/ML), EBS, and fleet-wide via ENA Express. Source: [A Cloud-Optimized Transport for Elastic and Scalable HPC, IEEE Micro 2020](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/9167399), [AWS ENA Express announcement 2022](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/aws/new-ena-express-improved-network-latency-and-per-flow-performance-on-ec2/).
- **[V-partial]** **Meta**: memcached gets moved to **UDP** for latency as far back as 2013 ([Scaling Memcache at Facebook, NSDI 2013](https://www.usenix.org/conference/nsdi13/technical-sessions/presentation/nishtala)); the widely-cited figures — UDP gets **~20% lower latency than TCP**, median get ~**333 µs** / 95th ~**1.135 ms** for representative web requests — are **[M]** (paper PDF fetch 403'd; verify before quoting). Meta's AI fabrics use RoCEv2 ([Meta engineering, 2024](https://engineering.fb.com/2024/08/05/data-center-engineering/roce-network-distributed-ai-training-at-scale/)) **[M]**.
- **[M]** Research shows software alone reaches the same niche on plain Ethernet: **eRPC (NSDI 2019)** — **2.3 µs median RPC**, ~10M small RPCs/s/core, no special transport ([paper](https://www.usenix.org/conference/nsdi19/presentation/kalia)).
- **[V-context]** The intellectual frame for this niche is Google's own **"Attack of the Killer Microseconds" (CACM 2017)** — the µs-scale I/O problem is real and was named a decade ago ([paper PDF](https://www.barroso.org/publications/AttackoftheKillerMicroseconds.pdf)); the industry's answer was offload+userspace stacks, not a new kernel transport.

**Implication (inference):** Homa's addressable market is the gap between "kernel TCP is too slow" and "we already run RDMA/SRD/Snap/UDP where it matters" — i.e., mid-tier shops with µs-critical workloads, too sophisticated to tolerate TCP tails, not sophisticated enough to run RoCE/kernel-bypass, yet able to re-port applications to a message-based API and (per Ousterhout's own numbers) willing to eat kernel-software overheads that cap the win at ~an order of magnitude below hardware paths. That is a thin slice, and none of the $100M/yr-scale operators are in it.

## 6. Bandwidth-vs-engineering economics: the "just buy more Ethernet" alternative

- **[V]** Ethernet $/bit keeps collapsing on schedule: **800G optics deliver ~25–30% lower cost-per-bit than two discrete 400G optics** (Cisco/Dell'Oro-sourced figures); 800G port shipments were forecast to **overtake 400G during 2025**, concentrated in AI back-end fabrics. Sources: [Lightwave/Dell'Oro](https://www.lightwaveonline.com/business/market-research/article/14279942/800g-data-center-switch-ports-to-top-400g-by-2025-delloro), [Cisco Live optics deck 2024](https://www.ciscolive.com/c/dam/r/ciscolive/global-event/docs/2024/pdf/BRKOPT-2699.pdf).
- **[V]** The data-center switch market **doubled in three years to a record 3Q 2025** — operators are buying bandwidth at unprecedented rates ([Dell'Oro, Dec 2025](https://www.delloro.com/news/data-center-switch-market-doubled-in-three-years-hits-record-3q-2025/)); 100/200/400G still make up more than two-thirds of front-end network sales ([Dell'Oro front-end note](https://www.delloro.com/news/data-center-switch-front-end-networks-expand-rapidly-to-keep-pace-with-ai-back-end-scale/)).
- **Framing (inference, no direct published TCO comparison exists — see Gaps):** with network at ~8–15% of TCO (§4) and $/bit falling ~25–30% per optics generation (§6), keeping utilization low by overbuying bandwidth costs roughly *(headroom multiple − 1) × 8–15% of TCO amortized over 5 years*, and that price falls every generation with **zero migration risk**. Adopting a fabric-wide new transport prices in: dual-stack operation, switch priority-queue configuration coupling, app rewrites to a message API (Homa is not a drop-in socket replacement — [arXiv:2210.00714](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.00714) **[V]**), retraining, and a decade of TCP-grade operational tooling (monitoring, load balancers, middleboxes) that doesn't understand the new protocol. This is the standard revealed-preference story: every hyperscaler that hit the µs wall chose offload point solutions + more bandwidth, and none adopted a fabric-wide TCP replacement for general traffic.
- **[M]** Hamilton (§4 source) makes the same directional observation: per-host bandwidth up ~100x while network stayed ~8–15% of all-in cost — bandwidth got cheap faster than transport engineering got easy.

## 7. Synthesis: the steel-man and where it breaks

**Steel-man of the $100M claim:** at Meta/Google scale, 0.2–0.3% of annual infra spend is $100M+; Homa demonstrably removes 7–83x of small-message P99 under load; latency-tier over-provisioning is real; therefore some efficiency gain × huge base ≥ $100M.

**Where it breaks, link by link:**
1. **Attribution:** published tail taxonomies (Dean/Barroso §1.1, Tales of the Tail §1.1) put host/app causes first; no operator study attributes a dominant share of end-to-end P99 to transport. Transport's recoverable µs–low-ms vanish inside 50–200 ms service budgets.
2. **Already harvested:** hedging captured 24x at the 99.9th for +2% load in 2013 (§2), in software, transport-untouched.
3. **Utilization premise false:** fabrics idle at ~1–25% average for burst/failure/growth reasons (§3); the binding µs-scale constraint (buffer microbursts) isn't solved by running hotter, and Homa's flat-tail evidence is testbed-scale only.
4. **Wrong cost slice:** network is 8–15% of TCO; Homa doesn't shrink the 45–57% server slice — offload does, and Azure/Google/AWS already did it (§4–5).
5. **Niche already served:** ~70% of Azure traffic is RDMA; Google runs Snap/Falcon; AWS runs SRD; Meta ran memcached-UDP in 2013 (§5).
6. **Cheaper alternative:** $/bit falls 25–30% per optics generation; buying headroom is a declining-cost, zero-risk substitute for transport migration (§6).

**What would change the verdict:** a hardware-offloaded Homa (the ATC'21 paper's own "another 5–10x" path) with demonstrated fleet CPU-core savings comparable to Snap's 3x Gbps/core, validated at 10k-node scale — at that point it competes with Falcon/SRD on the *server-cost* slice where $100M/yr is genuinely available. That product does not exist publicly as of 2026-07.
